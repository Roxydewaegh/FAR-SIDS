How do remittances and foreign aid shape wellbeing in island households?

FAR-SIDS is a two year study across Jamaica, Tonga, and Comoros, and the first to follow aid and remittances into the same households. It pairs econometric modelling with community led fieldwork and a household survey to understand dependency, agency, and self-reliance in Small Island Developing States.

The question

Two flows, one household

Small island developing states receive some of the world's largest flows of foreign aid and remittances relative to their size, and almost every study looks at one flow or the other. FAR-SIDS follows both into the same households and asks what each pays for: food, school, medicine, the house, water and electricity, giving to the church or mosque, family and customary obligations, and the tools people use to earn a living. If the two pay for different needs, they cannot stand in for one another, and a cut in one is not made up by the other.

The project deliberately begins from island agency and self-determination rather than from vulnerability. Island communities are treated as the experts on their own lives and economies. It studies three nations in three different oceans, each with a distinct history of migration, aid, and resilience. In each country the project begins by listening, in interviews and focus groups with community members, and then administers a household survey that records who paid for each of these needs over the past year and what happened the last time a payer changed.

The work is hosted at the Islands and Small States Institute at the University of Malta, itself a small island state, and runs with partner universities in each country studied.

The question

Why it matters

Foreign aid reaches a household as a programme, a school fee waiver or a free clinic that a donor funds and a government or agency delivers, and a remittance reaches a household as money that a relative abroad sends. Research has not yet examined what happens when aid and remittances meet in one household, and since 2025 the gap has become urgent. Aid from OECD donors fell by 23.1% in 2025, the largest annual drop recorded, and a further fall is expected in 2026 (OECD, 2026), so programmes that island households rely on may end as their funding is cut.

When a programme starts paying for a need that a relative abroad was paying for, the relative either stops sending money or keeps sending it and the household uses the money for something else. In the first scenario foreign aid replaces family support, and the household now relies on a programme decided and funded elsewhere for a need its own family was meeting. In the second scenario the relative keeps sending, and the household keeps support of its own while the programme adds to it. A programme can therefore replace family money or free it for a need the household chose, and what a household is left with when the programme ends follows from which of the two occurred.

FAR-SIDS records which of the two happens inside households in Jamaica, Tonga and the Comoros, so that a government or donor planning with a smaller budget can see which needs relatives abroad already pay for, which needs nobody pays for, and whether a programme replaces family money or frees it for another need.

The approach · the household survey

The household survey

The survey records aid and remittances in the same household. Its questions follow the national surveys each country already runs (the Jamaica Survey of Living Conditions, the Tonga Household Income and Expenditure Survey, and the Comoros household living conditions survey, known as the EHCVM), so the answers can be set beside national data.

At its centre is a payment matrix. For each item a household lives on, it asks who paid (family abroad, a programme, the church or mosque, or the household itself) and what happened the last time the payer changed. Between 250 and 300 households answer in person in each country.

The approach · ethnographic fieldwork

Semi-structured interviews

The interviews take place before the household survey. In each country, local research partners choose ten to twenty key informants, people who know the community well through their work or position. Each interview follows a list of questions but leaves room for the person’s own topics. It takes sixty to ninety minutes, in the person’s preferred language, with a local research partner present. In Tonga, interviews follow talanoa, a Pacific approach based on open conversation. Everyone signs a consent form, recording needs their permission, and each person checks their transcript before it is used.

Five questions go to every key informant

  1. “When a family here gets money from someone abroad, what happens to it? It arrives, and then, take me through it.”Establishes whether households keep money from different sources for different purposes, or put everything in one pot.
  2. “Think of the last time someone you know received money from abroad. Did the person who sent it say what it was for?”Establishes whether a stated purpose is binding here, negotiable, or only a courtesy.
  3. “Think of a family here that hit a hard month, the school fees due, or somebody sick. Where can they turn?”Shows where a struggling family actually turns in this community, whether to relatives, neighbours, a church, a savings group or a programme, under the names people here use for each.
  4. “Sometimes a programme, or the church, starts paying for something that a family used to get from relatives abroad. Have you seen that happen?”If yes, what happened next, and what the relative abroad did.
  5. “Think of a family here that people say is doing alright. What does that family have?”Followed by its own question: if all the money and help from overseas stopped coming tomorrow, what would happen here?

The approach · ethnographic fieldwork

Focus groups

The study runs three focus groups in each country: one for women, one for men and one mixed. Each has eight to ten people, invited through community leaders and local organisations. People who decide who receives a programme are not invited, nor are people whose presence would stop others from speaking. The study interviews them separately as key informants. Everyone signs a consent form. At the end, the note taker reads a summary back to the group, and the group corrects it before the notes are saved. Each session takes about two hours and has three parts.

Part one: what does a family here pay for?

The facilitator asks the group one question: what does a family here have to pay for? Each answer is written on its own card, such as food, school, the church, a funeral or the roof. The group keeps adding cards until it has nothing more to add.

Next, the facilitator places thirteen cards beside the group’s cards. These thirteen cards show the types of household spending that the household survey asks about. The group compares the two sets of cards and says which costs the survey left out and which ones it describes wrongly.

Part two of the exercise uses the group’s cards, not the survey’s cards. The results therefore show how people in the group describe their own household spending. Part one takes about fifteen minutes.

Part two: what each source of money pays for

The facilitator names one source of money at a time: family living abroad, work here, a programme, and the church or mosque. For each source the group receives one hundred beans and places them on its cards, with more beans on the costs that this source pays more of. The group argues until it agrees. The same hundred beans are picked up and used again for the next source. The facilitator records where the beans went and asks the group why.

Then come the moves. The facilitator points to a pile that a programme pays for and asks what would happen if that programme stopped tomorrow, and who would pay instead. The facilitator then points to a pile that family abroad pay for and asks what the relatives would do if a programme started paying for that cost: send the same amount, send less, or send the same amount for something else. The group moves the beans to show its answer.

Whether the beans move to another card or leave the ground is the distinction the whole study turns on. Part two takes about fifty minutes.

Part three: seven questions for discussion

Part three is a conversation without cards or beans. It takes about forty minutes. The facilitator asks the group these questions in this order:

  1. What does a family here need to have, and need to be able to do, before people say it has enough? Would relatives living abroad give the same answer?
  2. If all the money and help from overseas stopped tomorrow, what would happen here? What would families stop paying for first?
  3. There are two invented families of the same size. One has a well built house, a phone, a motorbike and a daughter working abroad. The other has a simpler house, nobody abroad and steady local work. Which family is doing better, and why? Surveys usually count what a family owns, such as the house, the phone and the motorbike. The group’s reasons show whether that count leaves out something people here think matters.
  1. A programme repairs a family’s water tank. Or a relative sends the family the same amount of money in cash. Which would families here prefer, and why? The facilitator then raises or lowers the cash amount and asks the question again.
  2. Which months of the year are the hardest for families here? In which months does money come in? When money runs short in the hard months, what do families do first?
  3. When a new source of money starts paying for a cost, such as school fees, whose work in the family changes? Whose work stays the same?
  4. Looking at all the cards and beans from today, what is missing? Which costs do families here get no help with at all? When help is offered here and someone does not take it, why?

Whether households keep money from each source separate

Does a household keep money from each source apart, or mix it all together? The survey asks what each source of money pays for. If the money is mixed, people cannot answer that question.

2

Whether households do what the sender asks

When a relative abroad says the money is for school fees, does the household feel it must spend it on school fees? If it does, a programme that starts paying the school fees may lead the relative to send less. The study calls this attachment.

The study learns the words people here use for having enough and for living decently, and the local names for what households pay for. It also learns the names of large events that cost families money, such as funerals, the misinale (the yearly giving to the church in Tonga) and the grand mariage (the large customary wedding in the Comoros).

A list of programmes in each community

The study lists every programme that works in each area, under the names people use for it. The survey then asks people about each programme by a name they know.

The approach · econometric models

Econometric models

The country models ask the survey’s question for the whole country: do aid and remittances replace each other, add to each other, or leave each other alone, and what does that mean for how people live? Each country has one figure a year, for 44 years in Jamaica (1980 to 2023), 31 in Tonga (from 1993) and 22 in Comoros (from 2002, or 43 once earlier years are filled from IMF and World Bank records).

The models, called autoregressive distributed lag (ARDL) models, separate a real long run relationship from two series that simply rise together, and they allow an effect to arrive years after the money. Remittances come from each central bank and aid from OECD records, and the country outcome, chosen with the supervisor, is household consumption per capita or satellite night lights. Each series is first checked against the years of storms, recessions and other shocks recorded in the two pages below.

Literature review · Reading the studies

How the forty-six themes were built

Every included study was read in full and entered as one row in a single table. The row records what the study measured, the method it used, the outcome it reported, how the money reached people, and where it disagrees with another study. Patterns were then coded across rows rather than within a single study. A pattern became a theme only where it appeared in more than one study and in more than one method. The list was checked against the table a second time, and four further readings were run without sight of it. The steps follow the six phases of thematic analysis set out by Braun and Clarke (2006).

Next, what the reading led us to argue

The literature review · hypothesis and claims

What we argue, and why

Remittance research grew out of migration studies and asks what households do with the money. Aid research grew out of public finance and asks what governments do with it. Across the 201 studies reviewed, no study observes one household receiving both. Xenogiani (2006) named the interaction between the two flows as an open question twenty years ago, and it is still open today.

Attachment: what a transfer is tied to

Outside support does not all arrive as free money. A daughter in Auckland sends money for the school fee and says so. A brother in Marseille pays the electricity bill directly, so the money never passes through the household at all. A programme pays a transfer on the condition that a child attends school. A congregation expects its annual contribution. In each case the money is tied to a purpose, a person, a rule or an obligation, and the giver can ask how it was used and give less if the answer displeases her. FAR-SIDS calls this property attachment. Sociologists have described the same habit as earmarking (Zelizer, 1994) and behavioural economists as mental accounting (Thaler, 1999): people treat money as belonging to a purpose. A dollar is a dollar at a bank, but inside a household a roof is not a school fee.

Attachment decides what the money pays for, who controls it, and whether it can be moved to another use. The question that matters is therefore not whether a transfer is attached but what it is attached to. A transfer can be attached to an item: the money exists to pay for one particular thing, so once somebody else pays for that thing, the reason to send it is gone. A transfer can also be attached to people: the money exists to support one particular household, so when somebody else covers one of its needs, the money moves to the next need. A programme transfer is attached to an item by construction, because a fee waiver waives a fee and a clinic provides consultations, and no administrator can redirect either to a roof. Whether a remittance is attached to the school fee or to the family that pays it cannot be seen in any national total, but it can be observed inside a single household.

Remittances are sent for a purpose

Tongan migrants in New Zealand were surveyed about the money they sent home. Nearly seven in ten recipients received cash that the sender had earmarked, at least in part, for a named purpose, most often a church fundraising event.

Gibson, McKenzie and Rohorua, 2006

Jamaican farming households reported that what the sender had asked the money to be used for shaped what it paid for, most strongly when sender and receiver were building or repairing a family house together, raising children, or paying farm workers.

Thomas-Hope, 2017

When the purpose disappears, the money moves rather than stops

In an ordinary year, rural households in Samoa put about 17% of the remittances they received to ceremonial obligations (fa’alavelave) and about 12% to the church. After the 2009 tsunami those shares fell to 9% and 9%, while food took 16% and health care 13%. The same senders sent the same money, and the households moved it to the emergency.

Le Dé, Gaillard, Friesen and Matautia Smith, 2015

In July 2020 Tonga’s borders closed and the church conferences, the Heilala festival and the Agricultural Show were all cancelled. Money sent for those events should have fallen with them. Instead, remittance receipts that month rose by 5.6 million pa’anga, or 17.4%, the highest monthly figure since 2014.

Vainikolo, 2021

In both cases the money kept coming while its use moved, which is what a transfer attached to people does and a transfer attached to an item cannot.

What happens to a remittance when an aid programme begins paying for something the sender was paying for?

If a transfer is attached to people, then when another payer takes over a need it was covering, the sender continues to send and the household applies the money to a different need. If a transfer is attached to an item, the sender reduces or stops. We predict that remittances behave in the first way, and that foreign aid behaves in the second.

Three decades of crowding out research has assumed the opposite about private transfers, on national totals, without once testing it inside a household.

The three claims the household survey tests

Claim 1

The map: who pays for what

For thirteen categories of household spending, from staple food to school fees, medicines, church giving and the tools of a living, the survey records which source (aid or remittances) paid most of those needs over the past twelve months and which other sources contributed. No existing survey records that for both flows in one interview, and the map is a finding on its own whatever the other two claims show.

What would show this claim is wrong

If most households say the money was all mixed together and they cannot tell which source paid for each type of spending, the claim is wrong. The survey includes this as a possible answer for every type of spending. In that case, the study would report that the survey cannot show who pays for what. This would complicate the study, because the map of who pays for what is the main result of the survey’s largest section.

Claim 2

Attachment

Attachment means that money comes with a purpose the household feels it must follow. The survey asks four questions to find out whether this happens. It asks them in the same words about three kinds of giver: relatives abroad, aid programmes, and relatives living elsewhere in the country. Did the giver say what the money was for? Did the giver expect anything from the household in return? Has the giver ever checked how the money was used? Has the giver ever given less, or stopped giving, because of what they found?

What would show this claim is wrong

If givers say what the money is for but do not check how it was used and do not give less, the claim is wrong. A purpose with no consequence for the household is not attachment. The claim is also wrong if households say they put all the money together, whatever it was sent for.

Claim 3

Reallocation: the payer change

This claim separates two kinds of attachment: attachment to a cost, such as school fees, and attachment to the people in the household. Households report whether, over the past three years, anything on the list changed payer or lost its payer. For the most recent change, the survey records who paid before, who pays now, what the earlier payer did with the money it used to give, and whether the new payer started before or after the earlier one changed. If the earlier giver kept giving and the household used the money for a different cost, the giver was attached to the people. If the earlier giver gave less or stopped, the giver was attached to the cost.

Limits set before fieldwork begins

A change counts only if the household keeps money from different sources separate, and if the new giver started before the earlier giver changed. The timing suggests the new giver led to the change. The study reports how many changes it finds but does not use these numbers to estimate how common this is in any country.

The study will revise these claims as it reads more research and tests the survey in a trial run (pilot).

Outreach

Public engagement

This page turns what is said and written about aid and remittances in the islands into short pieces that anyone can read. Some of it comes from newspapers, radio, podcasts and social media, and some from published research, including research behind paywalls. Each piece takes one source and says what it found, as researchers see it, as donors see it, and as island communities see it, so that the questions this study asks are open to everyone and not only to people inside a university.

Outreach · The listening post

The listening post

These are running threads of what island communities and their diasporas say in public about aid and money from away, one thread per island. Themes are paraphrased rather than quoted, private posts are never reproduced, and none of it is research data. The threads grow as the reading continues.

Outreach · Visualising stories

Research storytelling

Academic research rarely leaves academia in a form anyone else can use. Each entry here takes one study or report on aid, remittances and wellbeing and turns its main finding into a chart, a map or an illustrated figure, with the study named underneath. The stories run as four threads, one for each island and one for small island developing states as a whole. Open a thread to read it.

Outreach · Island voices

Participants tell it in their own words

This page belongs to the people the project works with. During fieldwork in Jamaica, Tonga, and Comoros, participants who choose to, and who give their consent, can share their stories here in whatever form they wish: photographs, artwork, video, or their own words. Over time it becomes a place where the islands publish their own account of aid, remittances and daily life. Nothing appears here until the fieldwork begins and the first participants choose to contribute.
