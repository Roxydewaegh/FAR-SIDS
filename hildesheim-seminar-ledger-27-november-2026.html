Opening · The question for today

A small state lives on money it did not make at home, and the records say how much arrived but not who decided what to do with it

Aid, or official development assistanceMoney that a foreign government, or an official body such as the World Bank, gives to a government or lends to it on cheaper terms than a bank would. Public money passing between governments.
A remittanceMoney that a relative working abroad sends home to the family. Private money passing between people.
A small stateFor today, a state whose home market is too small to make most of what it needs, so that it depends on what comes in from outside: trade, rent for a strategic position, aid, and the earnings of people who left.

Four bars, two countries in Europe and one in the Pacific. In 1973 Malta received net aid worth 7.5 per cent of its national income. In 1979 Portugal received remittances worth 9.3 per cent of its GDP, from the million and more Portuguese who had gone to France and Germany to work. In 2023 Tonga received aid worth 20.3 per cent of national income and remittances worth 38.6 per cent of GDP, the highest share in the world that year.

Every one of those bars is a source. Somebody counted something under rules, wrote the number down, and a database later published it. The question today is what those records can and cannot tell us: they say how much money arrived, and they do not say who decided how it was used, or what the family that received it gave up to get it. That gap between the record and the decision is where the history of a small state is written, and it is what your students will need to see if they are to read these numbers critically.

Part one · 12:20 to 12:40

Two kinds of money, and the rules behind the numbers

Aid and remittances are older than their measurement. The category came later than the flow, and the rules of the category decide what the archive of numbers contains.

Part one is about counting. For a historian this is familiar ground, because a statistic is a document with an author, a date and a purpose, and the two kinds of money we look at today were flowing long before anyone gave them a name and a ledger. Aid became a category with rules in the 1960s. Remittances became a line in national accounts at about the same time. Before those dates the same money moved as colonial grants, base rents, and letters with banknotes in them, and it left a different kind of trace.

Part one · What counts as aid

A payment counts as aid only if it meets four conditions, and the donor governments wrote those conditions in 1969 and report the figures themselves

1961

The Development Assistance Committee

The rich donor governments form a committee inside the OECD in Paris, the DAC, which still writes the rules for what counts as aid.

1969

Official development assistance defined

The DAC adopts the term and the definition. From this date a figure exists that can be compared across countries and years.

1972

The concessional test

The DAC tightens the rule on loans: only a loan on much cheaper terms than a bank would give counts. A loan at the market rate is not aid, whoever makes it.

The four conditionsIt comes from a government or an official agency. It goes to a country on the DAC list of recipients. Its main purpose is the development and welfare of that country, so military spending does not count. It is a grant or a cheap loan.
Who counts itEach donor reports its own spending to the OECD against these rules, so every aid figure is built from what the donors said they gave, for their own accounting.
For the historianMalta and Cyprus were on the recipient list until the 1990s. Portugal, Greece and Spain received Marshall Plan money before the category existed, so the 1948 to 1952 flows are in a different archive from the 1969 series.

OECD, Official development assistance: definition and coverage. The DAC was created in 1961 and adopted the ODA definition in 1969; the concessionality rule was tightened in 1972.

Aid has a formal definition, and the definition has a date. In 1961 the rich donor governments formed a committee inside the OECD in Paris, the Development Assistance Committee, and in 1969 that committee adopted the term official development assistance and the four conditions still in force. The payment must come from a government or an official agency. It must go to a country on the committee's list of recipients. Its main purpose must be the development and welfare of that country, which excludes military spending. And it must be a grant, or a loan on much cheaper terms than a bank would give, a rule the committee tightened in 1972.

Two things follow for anyone teaching this. The first is that the donors also do the counting: each government reports its own spending against these rules, so the figure is defined by the governments that send the money. The second is that the category is younger than the flow. Malta and Cyprus stayed on the list of recipients until the 1990s, so their aid appears in the series from 1960. Portugal, Greece and Spain received Marshall Plan money between 1948 and 1952, before the category existed, so that money sits in a different archive and does not appear in the series at all. When a student finds no aid to Portugal in the World Bank database, the absence is a fact about the category, not about the money.

Part one · What counts as a remittance

Central banks count remittances from what banks and transfer companies report paying out, so cash in a suitcase and a parcel of goods are never in the figure

The one year rule

Who counts as living abroad

A person abroad for a year or more counts as living in the other country, so what they send home is a transfer. A seasonal worker abroad for seven months still counts as living at home, so their whole pay is counted, whether or not they send it.

How the figure is built

Reports, not households

Banks and transfer companies report to the central bank what they paid out and the bank adds the reports together. Nobody asks a household what it received.

What is left out

Cash, goods and bills paid from abroad

Money brought home on a visit, a parcel of clothes or food, and a school fee paid directly from abroad never pass through a bank, so they are in no figure. The official number is the smallest amount a country could have received.

The balance of paymentsThe record every central bank keeps of the money that comes into a country and goes out of it in a year. Remittances are one line of it, defined by the International Monetary Fund's manual.
For the historianThe Maltese who sailed to Australia in the 1950s sent money by postal order and brought it back in their luggage; the Portuguese in France in the 1960s carried it home in August. Most of that money is in no series, and the letters, parcels and postal orders are the archive that survives.

IMF, Balance of Payments Manual, remittances annex.

Remittances are counted in a different way, through the balance of payments that every central bank keeps, which records all the money that came into a country and went out of it in a year. Remittances are one line in that record, and the line follows a rule from the International Monetary Fund's manual: a person abroad for a year or more counts as living in the other country, so what they send home is a transfer, while a seasonal worker abroad for seven months still counts as living at home, so their whole pay is counted whether or not they send it.

The figure is built from reports. Banks and transfer companies tell the central bank what they paid out, and the bank adds the reports together. Nobody asks a household what it received, so anything that never passes through a bank is missing: cash carried home on a visit, a parcel of goods, a school fee or a hospital bill paid directly from abroad. The official figure is therefore the smallest amount a country could have received, and the true amount is larger by an amount nobody knows. For the twentieth century the gap is wider still, because the Maltese in Australia and the Portuguese in France sent postal orders and carried banknotes home in August, and the letters and parcels are the archive that survives rather than the series.

Part two · 12:40 to 13:00

Whose decision?

A dollar of aid and a dollar of remittance have the same value, and different people decide how each is used. Malta in 1972 and Tonga in 2026 show the same two flows sixty years apart.

The stops in this part

  1. 9Five decisions or one person
  2. 10Malta, 1972
  3. 11Tonga, 2026
  4. 12What FAR-SIDS asks

Part two starts from an observation that sounds obvious and changes everything once it is taken seriously. A dollar of aid and a dollar of remittance are worth the same, and different people decide how each is used. Aid is decided far from the household and written down at every step. A remittance is decided inside the household and written down nowhere. Malta in 1972 and Tonga in 2026 show the same two flows, and in both the state bargained hard over one flow while its own citizens carried the other.

Part two · Who decides

Aid passes through five decisions before it reaches a household, whereas a remittance arrives with a named person

Ask the room

Where is the household asked?

At which step of the aid chain does anybody ask the household what it needs? At which step of the remittance chain is anything written down that a ministry, or a historian, could read?

The paper trail

Two archives of very different sizes

The aid chain leaves budget debates, agency strategies, agreements, appraisal documents and completion reports. The remittance chain leaves a transfer company's record of an amount, and whatever the family kept.

In the aid chain a donor parliament, in London, Canberra or Brussels, sets the budget, and the donor's agency sets the priorities, health one year and roads the next. The receiving government negotiates the project and often pays part of it. The project team writes the rule that decides which villages and which households take part. Some households qualify and some do not, and the one that qualifies usually receives a service, a clinic, a water tank or a road, and rarely receives cash.

The remittance chain is shorter. A family member abroad earns the money, the transfer company takes its fee, which is about eight in every hundred on the routes into Tonga today, and the money arrives with one named person, a mother, a sister or a grandmother, who decides inside the house what to do with it. Ask the room at which step of the aid chain anybody asks the household what it needs, and the honest answer is the fourth step at the earliest and often none. Then ask at which step of the remittance chain anything is written down, and the answer is none, apart from the amount. For a historian that asymmetry is the whole problem: the flow that was decided far from the household left a large archive, and the flow the household decided left almost none.

Part two · A European small state as bargainer

In 1972 Malta priced its harbour at 14 million pounds a year while its emigrants priced their own labour abroad, and only the first negotiation is in the archive

The state's flow

14 million pounds a year, seven years

On 26 March 1972 Malta signed a new defence agreement with Britain: 14 million pounds a year for the use of the base, paid by Britain and its NATO allies, after Dom Mintoff had threatened to offer the harbour elsewhere. Net aid reached 7.5 per cent of national income the next year.

The household's flow

More than 140,000 people gone

Between 1946 and the late 1970s more than 140,000 Maltese left on assisted passages, most to Australia, from a population of about 320,000. Remittances were 6.9 per cent of GDP in 1971, and the families who received them are in no negotiation record.

What is in the archive

One negotiation, not two

Kissinger's memorandum to Nixon of 11 March 1972 records Mintoff's demands to the pound. No memorandum records what a mother in Żejtun did with the postal order from Melbourne, or what she gave up when her son left.

Base agreement: Foreign Relations of the United States 1969 to 1976, volume XLI, document 247, Kissinger to Nixon, 11 March 1972, on the 14 million pound NATO offer and Mintoff's demands. Aid share: World Bank DT.ODA.ODAT.GN.ZS. Emigration: King (1979); Malta's assisted passage scheme ran from 1948.

Malta in 1972 is the case where a small state bargains. Dom Mintoff, prime minister from 1971, told Britain that the harbour it had used since 1800 had a price, and that Libya and others might pay it. The memorandum that Henry Kissinger wrote to President Nixon on 11 March 1972 records the position: Britain and its NATO allies had offered 14 million pounds a year, Mintoff wanted a further five million as a single payment and two smaller items worth about three million each, and Kissinger wrote that the central question remained money. The agreement was signed on 26 March 1972 for seven years, and Malta's net aid reached 7.5 per cent of its national income the following year. This is agency in the plainest sense: a small state pricing its position and being paid.

The other flow was carried by the emigrants. Between 1946 and the late 1970s more than 140,000 Maltese left on assisted passages, most of them to Australia, from a population of about 320,000, and the money they sent back was worth 6.9 per cent of GDP in 1971. The state built that flow too, through the passage scheme, and then recorded it as private. The archive is lopsided in a way your students should notice: the harbour negotiation is documented to the pound in Washington, London and Valletta, and the decision a mother in Żejtun made with the postal order from Melbourne, and what she gave up when her son left, is documented nowhere unless the family kept the letters.

Part two · A Pacific small state today

In Tonga today aid funds half the budget and remittances reach nine households in ten, and the government that designed the seasonal work scheme counts the money as private

The schemesNew Zealand's Recognised Seasonal Employer scheme, capped at 20,750 places a year, and Australia's Pacific Australia Labour Mobility scheme, which held 3,715 Tongans on 30 June 2026, 3,095 of them men. About one working Tongan man in five is in one of the two.
Aid or not?The OECD counts the training attached to the scheme as aid and excludes its running costs, because the scheme fills labour shortages in New Zealand and is an immigration policy. It states that migrants sending money home does not by itself make a programme aid.
Who receivesThe 2021 national survey found 90 in every 100 households receiving cash remittances, 96 in 100 of those headed by a woman, and a third of the money coming from each of New Zealand and Australia.

Now the same two flows in Tonga, a kingdom of about 104,000 people in the Pacific. New Zealand's plan for Tonga states that official development assistance made up about 51 per cent of the national budget in 2023 to 2024. Remittances were 39.2 per cent of GDP in 2024 on the World Bank series, and the national household survey of 2021 found 90 in every 100 households receiving cash remittances, 96 in 100 of the households headed by a woman.

Where does the money come from? Largely from schemes that governments designed. New Zealand's seasonal employer scheme is capped at 20,750 places a year, Australia's labour mobility scheme held 3,715 Tongans on 30 June 2026, and a journalist's estimate puts about one working Tongan man in five in one of the two. The OECD's own casebook says the training attached to the New Zealand scheme counts as aid and its running costs do not, because the scheme fills labour shortages in New Zealand, and it states in so many words that migrants sending money home does not make a programme aid. So a donor government designs a labour scheme, the scheme produces remittances, and the accounts record them as private, exactly as Malta's passage scheme did. The difference from 1972 is that this time the household can be asked, and that is what my project does.

Part two · The present tense of the question

FAR-SIDS asks whether it matters to a household that a transfer arrives attached to a person rather than to a programme, and asks the households themselves

The claim

Attachment

A remittance is attached to a person, who decides inside the house. An aid programme is attached to a rule, decided outside it. We argue that this attachment, not the amount, decides what the money can do for a household and what it costs the household to receive.

The three islands

Jamaica, Tonga, the Comoros

One island in each ocean, each with a former colonial power in Europe and a diaspora in a richer country, each receiving both flows on a large scale. Household surveys in six communities per country in 2027, and forty years of national figures.

What the twentieth century adds

The European cases as a past

Malta, Cyprus and Portugal have already lived the transition the islands are living now: from remittance economy to member of a rich club. Their archives are where the long run of the question can be read.

The FAR-SIDS website

My project takes this question into the present tense. FAR-SIDS runs from 2026 to 2028 across three islands, Jamaica in the Caribbean, Tonga in the Pacific and the Comoros in the Indian Ocean, each with a former colonial power in Europe, each with a diaspora in a richer country, and each receiving both flows on a large scale. We argue that what matters to a household is attachment: a remittance is attached to a person, who decides inside the house, while an aid programme is attached to a rule, decided outside it, and that difference, more than the amount, decides what the money can do for the household and what it costs the household to receive. In 2027 we ask the households, in six communities in each country, and we read forty years of national figures beside their answers.

Your seminar gives that question a past. Malta, Cyprus and Portugal have already lived the transition the islands are living now, from remittance economy to member of a rich club, and their archives are where the long run can be read.
