Good afternoon, and thank you, Yves, for the invitation. My name is Roxane de Waegh. I am Belgian, I did my doctorate in New Zealand on small islands in the Pacific, and I now work at the Islands and Small States Institute of the University of Malta on a Marie Skłodowska-Curie fellowship that studies how households in small island states live on money from outside, sent by relatives abroad or paid through foreign aid, in Jamaica, Tonga and the Comoros.

The picture on the right sets out the seminar on one axis. There are two lines, from 1945 to 2025, one for Malta and one for Jamaica, and on each line are the dated decisions we will follow. For Malta these are the 1956 vote to join Britain, independence in 1964, the harbour agreement of 1972, the closing of the British base in 1979 and membership of the European Union in 2004. For Jamaica they are the start of migration to Britain in 1948, the British law of 1962 that restricted entry, passed in the same year as Jamaican independence, the United States law of 1965 that opened entry to migrants with skills and education, and 2021, the year in which the money Jamaicans abroad sent home reached its highest share of the economy. The two islands left the same empire two years apart, and the question for today is what each could still decide for itself afterwards. The figures come later, at each decision, with their sources.

Your course asks how small states experienced the twentieth century, what opportunities and challenges they met, and which factors played a role. I answer with one European small state, Malta, where I live, in five stages, and then set Jamaica beside it. In the last part of the session we turn the same question to Germany, because most of you will soon teach this history in German schools.

The question for today is not whether small states were dependent. Malta in 1957 shows how dependent they could be: about 12,000 people worked in one British naval dockyard, and a British official wrote that if it closed, Malta faced economic ruin. My question is how much agency a small island's government and its families had, and by agency I mean something modest and precise: how far they could make their own decisions when a larger power set most of the terms.

Two kinds of money from outside run through the whole seminar. The first is remittances, the money emigrants send home to their families. In 1976, the first year for which the World Bank has figures for both islands, remittances were worth 6.1% of Malta's GDP and 3.0% of Jamaica's, so at that point they mattered more to Malta. GDP, gross domestic product, is the value of everything a country produces in a year. The second kind is aid, money that one government gives another. For Malta after independence it came as British and NATO payments that the Maltese government negotiated. We will see how the weight of these two kinds of money changed in each island, and who made the decisions about them.

Malta's century comes in five stages, in the order they happened, and each has a date, a number and a decision. In every stage I ask the same question: who made the decision? The first four stages follow the money. The fifth asks what the records let a historian see.

Stage one. For the first half of the century the British navy was Malta's main employer. In 1957 about 12,000 Maltese worked in the naval dockyard, on an island where the census that year counted 319,620 people. Malta imported most of its food, and British spending on the base, rather than Maltese exports, paid for those imports. Between 1940 and 1942 Italy and Germany besieged the island, and the Maltese depended on British convoys for food and fuel; in April 1942 Britain awarded the whole island the George Cross.

After the Suez crisis the British government cut its armed forces in the 1957 Defence White Paper. A British official wrote in a minute that if the dockyard were closed or run down, Malta faced economic ruin. The British government made that decision in London, and nobody in Valletta was asked. This stage gives us the first two conditions for the list we build today: a home market too small to employ its people, and dependence on one larger country.

Stage two. The colonial government answered a population it could not employ by paying people to leave. Russell King, working from the records of Malta's Department of Emigration, found that from 1946 more than 140,000 Maltese emigrated on assisted passages, paying only a token contribution to the fare. The chart shows where they went: 57.6% to Australia, 22% to Britain, 13% to Canada and 7% to the United States. The census of 1948 counted 305,991 people in Malta, so the emigrants equalled more than two in five of that population, and King calls Malta the European country with the highest rate of emigration after the war.

Two decisions sit side by side here. The government decided to pay the fares, and that decision appears in the records with its budget line. The families decided who would go and how much money to send home, and those decisions appear almost nowhere. The money did come back: on the World Bank series, remittances were worth 6.9% of Malta's GDP in 1971. That figure counts only transfers that passed through banks, not the postal orders, parcels and banknotes that emigrants carried home on visits. Keep these family decisions in mind, because the debate at the end of the session returns to them.

Smith, S. C. Stage three is the one your students will not expect. On 11 and 12 February 1956 Malta voted on whether to join the United Kingdom, with Maltese members in the House of Commons. The yes vote won 67,607 to 20,177, which is 77% of the votes cast, or three voters in four. The Nationalist Party and the Church had called for a boycott, turnout was 59%, and Simon Smith calculates that only 44% of the whole electorate voted yes. The British government called the result inconclusive.

The real obstacle was money. Dom Mintoff, the Labour prime minister, would only join Britain with what he called equivalence: health, education and social services comparable to Britain's, and equal wages for government employees. London costed that at about 10 million pounds a year and would not pay. Smith argues, convincingly in my view, that the British government had wanted integration to settle its constitutional and financial problems with Malta, and preferred to let Malta go once the cost was clear. Malta became independent on 21 September 1964 with a ten year defence treaty and what Dominic Fenech calls modest loans and grants.

This stage gives us the third condition as a question: does the state hold something a larger country needs, and can its government make that country pay for it? Malta held the harbour. In 1956 its government could not turn that into British money. In 1972 it could.

Stage four is the one moment in the century when Malta's government set the terms, and it is our clearest case of agency. Mintoff returned to office in 1971 and told Britain that it would now pay rent for the harbour it had used since 1800, and he named Libya and the Soviet Union as other possible tenants. Henry Kissinger's memorandum to President Nixon of 11 March 1972 records where the talks stood: Britain and its NATO allies had offered 14 million pounds a year, Mintoff wanted 5 million pounds more as a single payment, and in Kissinger's words the central question in the negotiations remained money. The agreement was signed on 26 March 1972 for seven years, and Fenech writes that the rent more than tripled. Simon Smith, who has read the British files for the years before, describes the Maltese as adept at manipulating, cajoling and even threatening the former imperial power to keep and increase its commitments.

The chart shows net official aid to Malta as a share of national income, from the World Bank. The line is already high in 1970, at 7.2%, because Britain was still paying the loans and grants agreed at independence. It dips to 4.7% in 1972, the year of the negotiation, and rises to 7.5% in 1973, the first full year of the new agreement. It then drifts down to 5.0% in 1979 and falls to 1.2% in 1980, after the base closed on 31 March 1979. The chart measures official aid only, so it shows the size and timing of Malta's outside public money, not every payment under the agreement, and the year to year changes after 1980 are too small to read much into.

The agreement had an end date. After 1979 Maltese governments built a new economy through tourism, manufacturing and, from 2004, membership of the European Union. So the third condition has two parts: a small state needs something a larger country wants, and its government needs to be able to make that country pay, which Malta's government did for seven years.

A decolonising question

Whose sources count?

A history written from the files is mostly a history of what Britain decided.

Smith (2007) and Smith (2023) on the British files. King (1979) on the records of Malta's Department of Emigration.

Stage five is about sources, where a historian's training and my own work meet. Look at the two rows. Every step on the aid row left paper: Smith wrote his articles from British Cabinet and Colonial Office files, Kissinger's memorandum is in the American archive, and the 1972 agreement has a text you can read. On the remittance row the record is thin. A central bank records the total amount that arrives through banks and transfer agents, which is where the World Bank figures come from. It does not record who sent the money, who in the family received it, what it paid for, or who decided any of this. Cash and parcels carried home by hand are not counted at all. King worked from the Department of Emigration's records, which tell us who left, when and for where, but not why.

So a history of Malta's dependency written from what survives is mostly a history of what the British government decided. That is not a criticism of Smith, who reads the files very well; it is a limit of the files. Decolonising that history, in the sense I use the word in my own work, means asking the people the decisions reached and treating what they say as evidence of the same standing as a minute written in Whitehall. That is why my own project asks Jamaican households directly, and Jamaica is where we go next.

After the war Jamaica followed the same route as Malta. From 1948 Jamaicans moved to Britain to work in the new health service, on the buses and in factories. On 1 July 1962, five weeks before Jamaican independence on 6 August, the Commonwealth Immigrants Act restricted that movement. Randall Hansen judges that the regime this act began was not racist in intention but racist in effect. In the same years Canada, in 1962, and the United States, in 1965, changed their laws to admit migrants on their skills and education, and Jamaicans turned there instead. The right hand chart shows the result for the 1970s, from the Migration Policy Institute: 256,984 Jamaicans emigrated to the United States, 56,964 to Canada and 13,831 to Britain. By 2008, when the Bank of Jamaica surveyed people who received money from abroad, 62% of the money came from the United States, 17% from Britain and 9% from Canada.

The left hand chart shows the money emigrants sent home as a share of GDP for both islands, from the same World Bank series and for the same years. In 1976, the first year with figures for both, remittances were worth 6.1% of Malta's GDP and 3.0% of Jamaica's, so they mattered more to Malta. After the British base closed in 1979, Maltese governments built new sources of income through tourism, manufacturing and, from 2004, membership of the European Union. As the Maltese economy grew, the money emigrants sent home became a small part of it, 0.5% of GDP by 1999. Jamaica's economy did not create enough work for its people, so Jamaicans kept leaving, and the money they sent home rose to 8.7% of GDP by 1999 and to 16% by 2024, after a peak of 23% in 2021.

Two cautions about the left hand chart. First, Malta's line jumps from 0.5% in 2003 to 5.2% in 2004 and falls from 2.1% in 2016 to 0.2% in 2017. These are most likely changes in how Malta records the money, the first in the year Malta joined the European Union, and I have not found a published explanation, so I read Malta's line only up to 2003. Second, Jamaica's peak came during the pandemic. Jamaica's GDP fell by 8.3% in 2020, while the money sent home rose from 2.6 billion US dollars in 2019 to 3.1 billion in 2020 and 3.7 billion in 2021, so its share of GDP jumped; as the economy recovered, the share fell back to 16% by 2024 although the amount stayed close to 3.6 billion dollars. The Jamaican government also still negotiates a great deal of the island's income from abroad: tourism, bauxite, loans and aid. What changed is that a large share of that income now also arrives as private transfers to households, and the government does not decide how those transfers are spent. In Malta, most income from abroad after 1979 came through tourism, exports and European membership, which governments could plan for and shape.

Now the question on the screen. At first most people say that money should go straight to families: it arrives quickly, nobody takes a share on the way, and the family decides what it needs. Then think about who is left out. In 2008, 43 households in 100 in Jamaica received money from abroad, so 57 in 100 did not. Money sent to a family pays for that family's needs, and the Bank of Jamaica found that frequent recipients spend most of it on food, school costs, utility bills and other living expenses. Money a government receives can pay for a school, a road or a clinic that every household uses, including households with nobody abroad, and a government can plan with it, which it cannot do with money that arrives in private hands. Let the room argue for a few minutes. There is no single right answer, and both positions have serious defenders.

For the last part of the session we take the question from Malta and Jamaica and turn it to a country you know well. On 20 December 1955 the West German labour minister Anton Storch and the Italian foreign minister Gaetano Martino signed the first recruitment agreement, in Rome. West Germany then signed agreements with Spain and Greece in 1960, Turkey in 1961, Morocco in 1963, Portugal in 1964, Tunisia in 1965 and Yugoslavia in 1968. According to the Federal Agency for Civic Education, about 14 million people came to work in West Germany by 1973, and just over 11 million of them went home again. On 23 November 1973 the federal labour minister, Walter Arendt, stopped recruitment, giving as his reason that the energy crisis might harm employment in the coming months.

The arrangement has the same parts as the story of Malta and Jamaica: a larger country that needs workers, smaller or poorer countries that send them, and families who decide who goes and what money comes home. So the question is the same one, asked of Germany: who benefited more, the receiving country or the sending countries, and who decided the terms? You will work on it in three groups, each from one side.

Open three breakout rooms and give each group twelve minutes. Each group argues from one side and answers the same three questions: what did its side gain, what did it lose, and who set the terms? The facts on the cards come from the Federal Agency for Civic Education, and each group should use them, together with what they already know about this history.

Group one takes West Germany's side. During the economic boom employers needed workers, and recruiting from structurally weaker regions abroad added workers without driving up wages. On 23 November 1973 the federal government stopped recruitment on its own decision. Answers I expect: West Germany gained labour and growth, and controlled when recruitment started and stopped; it lost the assumption that most workers would go home, because many stayed.

Group two takes the side of the sending governments, with Turkey as the example. Turkey had high population growth and serious underemployment, and it wanted foreign currency. Between 1961 and 1973 about 867,000 Turkish workers came to West Germany, and about 500,000 of them returned. In 1972 Turkish workers sent home 2.1 billion Deutschmarks, more than Turkey's trade deficit that year of 1.8 billion Deutschmarks. Answers I expect: Turkey sent away part of its unemployment and earned foreign currency, but its government signed the agreement and could not prevent the 1973 stop, and it lost many of its younger workers.

Group three takes the families' side. After the 1973 stop, many workers who were already in West Germany stayed and brought their families to join them. Between 1974 and 1979 the number of women from Turkey living in West Germany rose by about 21%, and the number of children under 15 doubled to about 420,000. Answers I expect: families gained wages and the money sent home, and paid for them with years of separation; after 1973 many decided to settle rather than return, which was a decision of their own, taken within terms that Bonn had set.

Give each group two minutes to report, in the order West Germany, sending governments, families. Then ask the whole room: who benefited more? Expect disagreement, and let it run, because the answer depends on what we measure. A state's accounts show growth and foreign currency. A family's life shows wages, separation and, for many, a new home.

Then ask the second question: who decided? Draw out that the decisions were unequal. The West German government signed the agreements and ended recruitment in 1973 on its own decision. The sending governments signed, but could not stop anything. Families decided who went, how much money to send home and, after 1973, whether to stay, and those decisions changed Germany more than the agreements themselves had planned. This is the same pattern we saw in Malta: the larger power set the terms, and governments and families made their own decisions within them.

Ask the room: the arrangement was the same kind, recruitment agreements, entry laws and money sent home, so why would it mean something different for a small island like Malta or Jamaica? Show the prompts, but let the students build the answer before you add yours.

Points to draw out if the room does not reach them. The first is scale. The chart compares two measures that are not identical, so read it as an order of size, not a precise comparison. More than 140,000 Maltese emigrated between 1946 and the 1970s, equal to 45.8% of the 305,991 people counted in the 1948 census. About 867,000 Turkish workers came to West Germany between 1961 and 1973, equal to 2.4% of Turkey's population of 36.6 million in 1970, and about 500,000 of them returned. A small island that loses that share of its people changes in a way a large country does not. The second point is the number of partners. Malta and Jamaica depended mainly on Britain, while a larger sending country could send workers to several countries at once. The third point is what happens when the partner stops: when Britain restricted entry in 1962 and closed its Malta base in 1979, neither island had a large economy at home to take the workers back. The fourth point is weight. Money sent home is 16% of Jamaica's GDP today, a far greater share of the whole economy than in most large countries.

The students may add points of their own, such as identity, the politics of a diaspora, or the size of government, and that is welcome. The two cards on the right show that scholars disagree about how to read all this. One tradition, the MIRAB model of Bertram and Watters, says that in the smallest islands migration, remittances, aid and government employment make up the economy, and reads that as dependence. Another, Katzenstein on Europe's small states and Baldacchino on islands, reads the same history and finds bargaining, including Malta's own choices in 1956 and 1972. This is where the three conditions from Malta help: the students can test them against Germany and see where they fit and where they do not.
