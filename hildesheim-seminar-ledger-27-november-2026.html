Opening · The question for today

A small island lives on money it did not make at home, and the century is the story of who decided what that money would be

Dependency

One partner, one employer, one market

In 1957 about 12,000 Maltese worked in the British naval dockyard, and a British official wrote that if it closed "Malta faces economic ruin" (Smith 2007, page 63).

Remittances

The money emigrants send home

In 1979, the year Malta lost its British rent, money sent home was worth 2.9 per cent of GDP in Malta and 3.7 per cent in Jamaica: about the same. By 2024 Malta was at 0.1 per cent and Jamaica at 16 per cent. Same empire, same starting point, opposite directions (World Bank).

Aid

Money one government gives another

Malta was paid for its harbour until 1979. Jamaica was offered a prison in 2015. Both are aid in the accounts, and neither was decided by the people it reached.

Agency, for todayThe room a small state and its households had to act, within terms set elsewhere. Not whether they were free, but what they did with the little freedom the situation gave them.
A small island developing stateThe United Nations' name for islands like Jamaica, Tonga and the Comoros. Malta was on the aid recipients' list until the 1990s and is in the European Union today; that journey is the comparison.

The question for today is not whether small states were dependent. They were, and Malta in 1957 shows how completely: about 12,000 people worked in one British dockyard, and a British official wrote that if it closed Malta faced economic ruin. The question is who decided what the money would be. Rent for a harbour, an assisted passage to Australia, a pound in a letter from Melbourne, a prison built with aid money in Kingston: each of these is a decision, and each was taken by somebody in particular.

So when the title says agency, I mean something modest and precise: what a small state, and the families in it, could still decide for themselves when the big decisions were taken elsewhere, and what they did with those choices. Malta and Jamaica both left the British empire in the early 1960s with mass emigration under way and one dominant partner, and in 1979 the money their emigrants sent home was worth about the same share of each economy, three per cent. Malta then priced its harbour, lost the rent, and rebuilt on tourism, manufacturing and the European Union; by 2024 emigrants' money had almost vanished from its accounts. Jamaica could not price anything Britain wanted, and by 2024 the money its emigrants sent home was worth 16 per cent of everything the island produced, after peaking at 23 per cent in 2021. That divergence is the seminar.

Part one · Move one, 1900 to 1957

Malta was a fortress economy: Britain's navy was the employer, the food came by ship, and a siege showed what one lifeline means

The employer

The dockyard

About 12,000 Maltese worked in the naval dockyard in 1957, on an island of about 320,000 people. British spending on the base, not Maltese exports, paid for the imports.

The lifeline

1940 to 1942

Under siege from Italy and Germany, Malta depended on convoys for food and fuel; the island received the George Cross in April 1942. Dependence on one external partner was not an abstraction, it was the bread ration.

The decision that was not Malta's

The 1957 Defence White Paper

Britain cut its forces after Suez. A British official wrote that if the dockyard were closed or run down, "Malta faces economic ruin" (Smith 2007, page 63). Nobody in Valletta had been asked.

Move one. For the first half of the century Malta was a fortress economy, and I mean that in the plainest sense: the British navy was the employer, and about 12,000 Maltese worked in its dockyard in 1957, on an island of about 320,000 people. The food came by ship, paid for by British spending on the base rather than by anything Malta exported. Between 1940 and 1942 the island was besieged by Italy and Germany, and its survival depended on convoys getting through with grain and fuel; Britain gave the whole island the George Cross in April 1942. Dependence on one external partner was the bread ration.

The decision that ended this was not Malta's. After the Suez crisis Britain cut its forces in the 1957 Defence White Paper, and a British official wrote in a minute that if the dockyard were closed or run down, Malta faced economic ruin. That sentence is in the British archive because the British wrote it. Nobody in Valletta was asked, and that is the first factor for your list: one partner, deciding.

Part one · Move two, 1946 to the 1970s

The colonial government paid people to leave, more than 140,000 went, and the money they sent home became Malta's private income

The assisted passageMigrants paid "only a token contribution to the air or boat fares" (King 1979, page 245). The state chose emigration as the answer to a population it could not employ, and Malta became "the European country with the highest rate of post-war emigration".
What came backRemittances were 6.9 per cent of GDP in 1971 on the World Bank series, and the parcels, postal orders and banknotes carried home on visits are in no series at all.
The household's decisionWho left and who stayed, what was sent and what it paid for, were decided in kitchens in Żejtun and Melbourne. The state's decision to fund the passage is in the archive; the family's is not.

King, R. (1979), The Maltese migration cycle: an archival survey, Area 11(3), 245 to 249, page 245. Jones, H. R. (1973), Modern emigration from Malta, Transactions of the Institute of British Geographers 60, 101 to 119. World Bank, personal remittances received, share of GDP.

Move two. Malta answered the population it could not employ by paying people to leave. Russell King, working from the Department of Emigration's own records, found that from 1946 over 140,000 Maltese emigrated through the assisted passage scheme, paying only a token contribution to the fare: 57.6 per cent to Australia, 22 per cent to Britain, 13 per cent to Canada and 7 per cent to the United States. That is more than two people in five of the 1946 population, and King calls Malta the European country with the highest rate of post-war emigration.

What came back is the money. On the World Bank series the transfers Maltese emigrants sent home were worth 6.9 per cent of GDP in 1971, and that figure only counts what passed through a bank; the postal orders, the parcels and the banknotes carried home on a visit are in no series. Notice the two decisions here. The state decided to fund the passage, and that decision is in the archive with its budget line. The family decided who left, what was sent and what it paid for, and that decision is nowhere, unless the family kept the letters. Hold that gap, because part three turns on it.

Part one · Move three, 1956 to 1964

In 1956 three Maltese voters in four chose to join Britain, Britain said no, and independence in 1964 came on London's terms

11 and 12 February 1956

The referendum

Question: did voters approve the integration proposals in the Government Gazette of 10 January 1956. Yes 67,607 (77 per cent of votes cast), no 20,177, turnout 59 per cent; the Nationalist Party and the Church urged a boycott. Less than half the electorate, 44 per cent, voted yes (Smith 2007, page 56).

The price Mintoff set

Equivalence

Dom Mintoff would join Britain only with "comparable health, educational and social services; equivalent wages" (Smith 2007, page 52). London costed that at about 10 million pounds a year (page 57) and would not pay.

What decolonisation looked like here

Independence as the cheaper option

Britain "saw integration primarily as a means of solving the complex constitutional and financial problems" of Malta, and let it die on cost. Malta became independent on 21 September 1964 with a ten year defence treaty and "modest loans and grants" (Fenech 1997, page 155).

Factor three, stated as a questionMalta's only asset that Britain wanted was the harbour. Could the island price it? In 1956 the answer was no; Britain preferred to leave. In 1972 the answer changed.
For the teacherA decolonisation in which the colony voted to stay and the empire declined. Ask a class what that does to the story of independence they were taught.

Smith (2007), pages 52, 56, 57 and 66. Fenech, D. (1997), Malta's external security, GeoJournal 41(2), 153 to 163, page 155. Austin, Dennis (1971), Malta and the End of Empire, Frank Cass.

Move three, and the one your students will not expect. On 11 and 12 February 1956 Malta voted on whether to join the United Kingdom, with Maltese members in the House of Commons. Three voters in four said yes: 67,607 to 20,177. But the Nationalist Party and the Church had called for a boycott, turnout was 59 per cent, and Simon Smith calculates that less than half the electorate, 44 per cent, actually voted yes. Britain called the result inconclusive.

The real obstacle was money. Dom Mintoff, the Labour prime minister, would only join Britain with what he called equivalence: comparable health, education and social services, and equal wages for government employees. London costed that at about ten million pounds a year and would not pay it. Smith's argument, and I find it convincing, is that Britain had wanted integration as a way to settle its constitutional and financial headaches with Malta, and once the price was clear it preferred to leave. Malta became independent on 21 September 1964, with a ten year defence treaty and what Dominic Fenech calls modest loans and grants. So this is a decolonisation in which the colony voted to stay and the empire declined, because staying cost more. Ask a class what that does to the story of independence they were taught, and you have a lesson.

Part one · Move four, 1971 to 1979

In 1972 Mintoff sold the harbour to Britain and NATO for 14 million pounds a year, the only time Malta set the price, and in 1979 the base closed

The negotiationKissinger to Nixon, 11 March 1972: Britain and NATO offered 14 million pounds a year; Mintoff wanted five million more as a single payment; "the central question in the negotiations remains that of money". Signed 26 March 1972 for seven years; the rent "more than tripled" (Fenech 1997, page 156).
The methodSmith (2023) on 1964 to 1971: "the Maltese proved adept at manipulating, cajoling, and even threatening the former imperial power to maintain and even increase its commitments." Mintoff named Libya and the Soviet Union as alternative tenants; NATO moved its headquarters to Naples.
The end"In 1979 the British base closed down peacefully and all occupied lands were returned to the Maltese Government" (Fenech 1997, page 156). Net aid fell from 5 per cent of national income in 1979 to 1.2 per cent in 1980, and Malta had to become something else: tourism, manufacturing, and in 2004 the European Union.

Move four is the one moment in the century when Malta set the price. Mintoff returned to office in 1971 and told Britain that the harbour it had used since 1800 had a rent, and that Libya and the Soviet Union might pay it. Henry Kissinger's memorandum to President Nixon of 11 March 1972 records where things stood: Britain and its NATO allies had offered 14 million pounds a year, Mintoff wanted five million more as a single payment, and, in Kissinger's words, the central question in the negotiations remained money. The agreement was signed on 26 March 1972 for seven years. Fenech says the rent more than tripled; NATO moved its Mediterranean headquarters to Naples. On the World Bank series, aid to Malta reached 7.5 per cent of national income the next year.

Simon Smith, who has read the British files for the years before this, describes the Maltese as adept at manipulating, cajoling and even threatening the former imperial power to keep and increase its commitments. That is what I meant at the start: the big terms were set in London and Brussels, but the price of the harbour was still Malta's to set, and Mintoff set it. It had a term, though. On 31 March 1979 the base closed, peacefully, and the land came back; aid fell from 5 per cent of national income to 1.2 per cent in a year, and Malta had to become something else, which it did, through tourism, manufacturing and, in 2004, the European Union. The third factor, then: a strategic position is an asset only while somebody wants it, and only if the island can price it.

Part one · Move five · Decolonising the story

The archive kept the state's negotiations to the pound and the household's decisions not at all, so the history of Malta's dependency is mostly the history of London's side of it

Minutes, memoranda, agreements

What does not

Why this is a decolonising question

Whose sources count

A history of Malta's dependency written from what survives is a history of what Britain decided. Decolonising it means going to the people the decisions reached and asking, which is what my project does in Jamaica, Tonga and the Comoros.

Smith (2007) and Smith (2023) on the British files. King (1979) on the emigration cards, the one household-level record that does survive. On method: the FAR-SIDS household survey, 2027.

Move five is about the sources, and it is where a historian's training and my own meet. Look at the two rows. Every decision on the aid row left paper: Smith wrote his articles from Cabinet and Colonial Office files, Kissinger's memorandum sits in the American archive, the 1972 agreement has a text you can read. The remittance row left almost nothing. Who in the family decided that the son would go to Melbourne, what the first postal order paid for, what the mother gave up when he left: none of that was written down, unless the family kept the letters. King found one exception, the emigration cards the Maltese state kept from 1976, and they record name, date and destination, not reasons.

So a history of Malta's dependency written from what survives is, for the most part, a history of what Britain decided. That is not a criticism of Smith, who reads the files superbly; it is a fact about the files. Decolonising that history, in the sense I use the word in my own work, means going to the people the decisions reached and asking them, and treating what they say as evidence of the same standing as a minute in Whitehall. In Jamaica, Tonga and the Comoros that is what my project does, and Jamaica is where I take you next, because it left the same empire two years before Malta.

Part two · Jamaica beside Malta

Jamaica left the same empire in 1962, sent its people to the same Britain, and could price nothing Britain wanted, so its century ran the other way

1948 to 1962

The door opens, then closes

Caribbean migrants arrived in Britain from 1948 to staff the health service and the buses. The Commonwealth Immigrants Act took effect on 1 July 1962; Jamaica became independent on 6 August 1962. Hansen calls the regime "not racist in intention, but racist in effect".

1979 to 2024

Remittances take over

In 1979 money sent home was 3.7 per cent of GDP, the same order as Malta's 2.9. It rose to 23 per cent in 2021 and was 16 per cent in 2024, while Malta's fell to 0.1. By the 1990s formal remittances "exceeded that of the traditional foreign currency earners of bauxite and sugar" (Thomas-Hope 1999). In 2008, 43 households in 100 received them; in 2010, three recipients in four were women (Bank of Jamaica 2011).

30 September 2015

A prison instead of reparations

CARICOM had adopted a ten point reparations plan in March 2014 (Strecker 2017). Visiting Kingston, David Cameron told parliament the region could "move on from this painful legacy", announced a 300 million pound Caribbean infrastructure fund, and offered 25 million pounds of aid to build a 1,500 bed prison so that Jamaicans in British jails could serve their sentences at home. Jamaica did not take the prison.

Reading the two linesSame source, same years, same unit, so the comparison is fair. Read the shape, not the small moves. Jamaica climbs from the 1990s as its emigrants settle in the United States and Canada. Malta fades to nothing by the 2000s as its emigrants' children stop sending money home. Two features of the Malta line coincide with changes in how Malta reports its accounts and should not be read as events: the jump in 2004, the year Malta joined the EU and moved to European statistical standards, and the drop in 2017. Jamaica is the case that tests the three factors, because it shares two of them with Malta and lacks the third. Caribbean migrants arrived in Britain from 1948 to staff the new health service and the buses, and Jamaica sent more than any other island. Then the door closed: the Commonwealth Immigrants Act took effect on 1 July 1962, and Jamaica became independent five weeks later, on 6 August. Randall Hansen's judgement on that regime is that it was not racist in intention but racist in effect. The emigrants turned to the United States and Canada, and the money they sent home rose from under 4 per cent of Jamaica's GDP in 1979 to 23 per cent in 2021, 16 per cent in 2024. Look at the chart: in 1979 the Malta line and the Jamaica line sit almost on top of each other, and from the 1990s they part. Elizabeth Thomas-Hope notes that by the 1990s remittances through the banks alone exceeded what sugar and bauxite earned. In 2008, 43 households in a hundred received them, and in 2010 three recipients in four were women.

Now the aid side, and the decolonising question in its sharpest form. In March 2014 the Caribbean governments adopted a ten point plan for reparations for slavery. On 30 September 2015 David Cameron, the first British prime minister to visit in fourteen years, told the Jamaican parliament that the region could move on from this painful legacy, announced a 300 million pound infrastructure fund for the Caribbean, and offered Jamaica 25 million pounds of aid to build a 1,500 bed prison, so that Jamaican prisoners in Britain could be sent home to serve their sentences. A Jamaican member of parliament called it an insult, and the prison was never built. Three years later the Windrush scandal showed people who had come from the Caribbean as children being detained and deported for lack of papers they had never been given. Malta could price its harbour; Jamaica had sugar, bauxite and people, and Britain wanted none of them enough to pay rent. That is why the two lines on this page run in opposite directions. One caution on reading the Malta line: the jump in 2004 and the drop in 2017 coincide with changes in how Malta reports its accounts, and I have not found a published explanation, so I read the shape of the line and not those two moves.

Part two · The present tense

FAR-SIDS goes to the Jamaican households the two flows reach and asks what each lets them decide, which is the question the archive could not answer for Malta

The claim

Attachment

A remittance is attached to a person, who decides inside the house what it pays for. An aid programme is attached to a rule, decided outside it, that says who qualifies. We argue that this attachment, more than the amount, decides what the money can do for a household and what it costs the household to receive.

The method

Relationship, reciprocity, respect

Six communities of fifty households in three parishes, interviewed in 2027 with local research partners, and forty years of national figures read beside their answers. The household's account of the money has the same standing as the ministry's.

Why Jamaica needs it now

Hurricane Melissa, 28 October 2025

After the storm, insurance payouts reached the treasury within days, aid reached ministries within weeks, and remittances reached named people at once. Nobody has yet asked whether the households that receive remittances recovered faster. That is answerable, and we will ask.

This is where my project enters, and it enters as method rather than as advertisement. FAR-SIDS argues one thing about the two flows: that what matters to a household is attachment. A remittance is attached to a person, who decides inside the house what it pays for. An aid programme is attached to a rule, decided outside the house, that says who qualifies and for what. We argue that this attachment, more than the amount, decides what the money can do for a household and what it costs the household to receive it. In 2027 we test that in six communities of fifty households in three Jamaican parishes, with local research partners, on the principles of relationship, reciprocity and respect, and we read forty years of national figures beside what the households tell us.

Why does this belong in a history seminar? Because it is the bottom row of the diagram on the last stop, written down at last. If we can record in 2027 who in a Kingston household decided what the money from Florida paid for, then the silence in Malta's archive is not a fact about households; it is a fact about who was asked. And Malta gives the project something in return: a small island that was a remittance and aid economy within living memory and is now in the European Union, which is the long run a two year project cannot see. Part three · Report back

The same three factors ran through all four lessons, and only Malta in 1972 had the third

Factor one

A home market too small to employ its people

Malta paid 140,000 people to leave. Jamaica's emigrants sent home a fifth of GDP. Both states chose emigration because the alternative was unemployment at home.

Factor two

One dominant external partner

Britain decided the dockyard in 1957, the integration in 1956, the door in 1962, the prison in 2015. The small state was consulted at the end, if at all.

Factor three

A position that can be priced

Only Malta in 1972 had something Britain and NATO would pay rent for, and only for seven years. Jamaica had people, and Britain closed the door on them.

One sentence to take away. A small state's century is the record of who decided what its money would be, and the household's half of that record is still mostly unwritten, in Malta because nobody asked, and in Jamaica because we are only asking now. Three short readings are on the screen, two of them open access, and my address is there for the four packs, which you are welcome to use in your own classrooms, and for the Jamaican results when they come. An alternative design for part three · B

Design B: each room gets a state document and a household document for the same moment, and teaches the class whose story the two tell

Room 1

Malta 1972

Kissinger's memorandum beside King's emigration figures: the state negotiating to the pound, and 140,000 people who had already left without a negotiation.

Room 2

Malta 1956

Smith's account of the equivalence demand beside the referendum result: what Mintoff asked for in London, and what 67,607 people voted for at home.

Room 3

Jamaica 2015

Cameron's speech to parliament beside the Bank of Jamaica survey: a prime minister offering a prison, and 43 households in 100 living on money from relatives, three quarters of the recipients women.

Room 4

Jamaica 1962

Hansen on the 1962 Act beside Thomas-Hope on remittances overtaking sugar: the law that closed the door, and what the people who had gone through it sent back for thirty years.

The lesson, five minutesShow both documents. Ask the class who is speaking in each, who is missing, and which one a textbook would quote. End on one sentence about what a history written only from the first document would get wrong.
When to prefer B over AWhen Yves's students have already done source criticism in his first two sessions and would find a role-play vote too simple. B is harder and quieter; A is livelier and works with mixed prior knowledge, which is what he described.

Design B keeps the same four moments and the same packs but changes the task from a decision to a comparison. Each room gets two documents for its moment, one from the state and one from or about households, and teaches the class whose story each tells: who is speaking, who is missing, and which one a textbook would quote. It ends on one sentence about what a history written from the first document alone would get wrong. It is the more demanding design, closer to what history students do in a source seminar, and I would choose it if Yves tells me the group has already worked on source criticism in his opening sessions.

An alternative design for part three · C

Design C: each room writes the letter that came with the money, using only what the pack allows it to know, and the class reads what the archive never kept

Room 1

Melbourne to Żejtun, 1972

A Maltese emigrant writes home the week the base agreement is signed, with a postal order enclosed. The pack gives the passage scheme, the wage gap, the news from Valletta.

Room 2

Birmingham to Kingston, July 1962

A Jamaican nurse writes home the week the Commonwealth Immigrants Act takes effect and five weeks before independence. The pack gives the Act, the health service, the money order.

Room 3

Kingston to a daughter in Florida, October 2015

A mother writes after Cameron's visit, about the prison, the reparations plan and what last month's transfer paid for. The pack gives the speech, the survey figures, the household budget shares.

Room 4

Valletta, 1956

A dockyard worker writes to a brother in Australia before the referendum: join Britain or not, and why. The pack gives the equivalence demand, the wages, the boycott.

The ruleEvery fact in the letter must come from the pack, with the source noted in the margin. The letter may invent feelings and names; it may not invent numbers, dates or events.
The lessonThe room reads its letter aloud, then the class says which sentences a historian could verify and which it could not, and what that gap means for how small states' histories get written. This is design A's reveal turned into design B's question, with the household's voice written by the students themselves.
When to prefer CWhen the group is confident and Yves wants the decolonising point to land hardest. It is the most creative and the riskiest: a room that writes a bad letter has still done the reading, but the class learns less.

Design C is the most creative of the three and the one that makes the decolonising point most directly. Each room writes the letter that came with the money: from Melbourne to Żejtun in the week of the 1972 agreement, from a Jamaican nurse in Birmingham in the week the 1962 Act took effect, from a Kingston mother to her daughter in Florida after Cameron's visit, or from a dockyard worker to his brother in Australia before the 1956 vote. The rule is that every fact in the letter comes from the pack with its source in the margin; feelings and names may be invented, numbers, dates and events may not. The room reads the letter aloud, and the class says which sentences a historian could verify and which it could not, and what that gap means for how the history of small states gets written. The household's voice, missing from the archive, is written by the students, and then interrogated as a source. I would use it with a confident group.
