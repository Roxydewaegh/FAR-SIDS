Marie Skłodowska-Curie Postdoctoral Fellowship · University of Malta · 2026 to 2028

How do remittances and foreign aid shape wellbeing in island households?

FAR-SIDS is a two year study across Jamaica, Tonga, and Comoros, and the first to follow aid and remittances into the same households. It pairs econometric modelling with community led fieldwork and a household survey to understand dependency, agency, and self-reliance in Small Island Developing States.

02

Two years, three phases

Year one runs from Malta and covers the reading, the training, the teaching, the partnerships and the ethics applications. Year two is fieldwork and secondments in Jamaica, Tonga and Comoros. The models, the results, the policy briefs and the papers follow in 2028.

First, in one minute, what the project is. FAR-SIDS is a two year study, from June 2026 to June 2028. It is funded by the European Union and hosted at ISSI, which is itself an institute of a small island state. It works in three countries in three oceans: Jamaica in the Caribbean, Tonga in the Pacific and Comoros in the Indian Ocean. In each country there is a partner university. This year, in Malta, I read everything already published, trained, built the partnerships, designed the household survey and submitted the ethics applications; in the autumn I teach at ISSI and at Hildesheim, and in December I present the review at DevNet in Wellington. Next year is the islands: about two months in each country, with interviews and focus groups first, then a household survey, and a seminar at the partner university before I leave. In 2028 the survey, the interviews and one econometric model per country are read together. An econometric model is a statistical method that takes one figure a year, for aid, for remittances and for how people live, and tests whether they have moved together over the decades. The findings go into policy briefs tested with governments and donors, a newspaper article in each country, open data, and three papers.

03

Two kinds of money reach island households

Remittances

Money that people working abroad send home to their families: a daughter in Auckland, a brother in Marseille, a son in Brooklyn.

Foreign aid

Money that governments and international organisations give to other countries. It reaches a household through a programme: a cash transfer, a scholarship, a water tank, a school meal.

Why it matters

Worldwide, families sent about 700 billion US dollars home in 2025 while official aid fell to about 160 billion. Small islands receive some of the largest flows of both, relative to the size of their economies, so what the two kinds of money do to each other matters more here than anywhere.

The project is about two kinds of money that reach island households from overseas. The first is remittances. Remittances are money that people working abroad send home to their families. The second is foreign aid. Foreign aid is money that governments and international organisations give to other countries. Aid reaches a household through a programme: a cash transfer, a scholarship, a water tank, a school meal. Worldwide the two kinds of money are not the same size. In 2025 migrants sent about 700 billion US dollars home to their families. In the same year official aid fell to about 160 billion. Small island states receive some of the largest flows of both, relative to the size of their economies, of any group of countries in the world, so what the two kinds of money do to each other matters more here than anywhere.

So what do we know about how these two kinds of money behave? This summer I read 201 studies for a systematic review. I followed a method called PRISMA, which records every search and every decision, so that somebody else could repeat the review and get the same result. The studies fall into two groups that rarely meet. Here is the first group, the remittance studies. They grew out of migration research, so they ask what families do with the money. They find that the money goes on food, school fees, medicine and the house, and that it often arrives with a purpose attached. What 201 studies say · the second group

The aid studies ask what governments do with the money

Poverty and child mortality

Across 26 countries, nine of them Caribbean islands, remittances were associated with lower poverty and lower child mortality; aid showed no statistical relationship with either.Borja 2020

The exchange rate

Across nineteen small island states, aid pushed up local prices and the local currency, the pattern economists call Dutch disease, so exporters sold less; remittances did not.Amuedo-Dorantes, Pozo and Vargas-Silva 2007

Who each flow reaches

Remittances go straight to households; aid goes to governments first, and much of what governments spend reaches better-off people and businesses. The part that reaches households comes through a programme, aimed at the poorest.Behzadan and Chisik 2024

Dutch disease in one example

A large aid project brings US dollars to Tonga. To spend them, the dollars become pa'anga and chase the same builders, drivers and land, so prices and wages rise, and the pa'anga strengthens. A farmer selling squash to Japan now has higher costs at home and a dearer price abroad, and sells less. Remittances are spent largely on imported goods, paid for abroad, so they do not chase the island's builders and land in the same way.

Why it matters

The two flows behave differently on almost every outcome measured.

The second group, the aid studies, come from somewhere else entirely. They grew out of public finance, so they ask what governments do with the money: how donors decide who gets it, whether countries can absorb it, and whether it produces growth. A few studies have put the two kinds of money side by side on the same outcome, and in those studies the two behave differently. Borja looked at 26 countries, nine of them Caribbean islands. Remittances went with lower poverty and lower child mortality. Aid showed no statistical relationship with either. Amuedo-Dorantes and colleagues looked at nineteen small island states and found something economists call Dutch disease. Let me explain that with an example. Suppose a large aid project arrives in Tonga. It brings US dollars, and to spend them in Tonga the dollars have to be changed into pa'anga and spent on Tongan things: builders, drivers, office staff, land, rent. The island has only so many builders and so much land. When a lot of new money chases the same builders and the same plots, their prices and wages go up. At the same time, all those dollars being changed into pa'anga make the pa'anga stronger, so one pa'anga now buys more dollars than before. Now think about a farmer who sells squash to Japan, or a hotel that sells rooms to Australians. Their costs at home have gone up, and a foreign buyer now needs more dollars to pay the same price in pa'anga. Their squash and their rooms have become dearer abroad, and they sell less. That is Dutch disease. In the island study, aid produced this effect and remittances did not, most likely because families spend remittances largely on imported goods, a fridge, a motorbike, rice, which are paid for abroad and do not chase the island's builders and land. Behzadan and Chisik add a second difference. Remittances go straight to households. Aid goes to governments first, and much of what governments spend ends up with better-off people and businesses. The small part of aid that reaches households directly comes through a programme, and programmes are aimed at the poorest. So the two flows behave differently on almost every outcome measured.

Why it matters

Aid fell 23 per cent in 2025 on the assumption that families will fill the space, and no study has tested that assumption inside a household. The countries where both flows are large enough to test it are almost all small islands: Tonga, Tuvalu and Comoros sit in the top twenty for aid and for remittances at the same time.

So here is the gap. Two groups of studies look at the same island economies, cite different papers and rarely meet. Of the 201 studies, eight put both kinds of money into the same model, and all eight use national totals. Not one follows aid and remittances into the same households. In 2006 Xenogiani set out the two possibilities, that the two kinds of money replace each other or that they add to each other, and concluded that nobody had settled it. Twenty years later nobody has. Why does that matter now? In 2025 the rich countries cut aid by 23 per cent, the largest cut on record, and the working assumption in policy circles is that family money will fill the space. And the countries where both kinds of money are large enough to test it are almost all small islands: Tonga, Tuvalu and Comoros are in the top twenty for aid and for remittances at the same time. This is an island question, and islands are where it can be answered.

Why it matters

The review suggests that none of the three holds. If so, the assumption behind the aid cuts fails, and small islands are the places where it fails first.

For family money to replace aid, three things would have to be true, and the review suggests that none of them holds. First, the two kinds of money would have to buy the same things. The studies that compare them find they behave differently on almost every outcome measured. Second, family money would have to be able to grow when aid shrinks. In the largest study of island economies, when aid went up, remittances went down, so the two already move together rather than one waiting to fill in for the other. And a family cannot simply decide to send more, because the money comes from people with jobs abroad, and the number of islanders allowed to work in Australia and New Zealand is fixed each year by those governments. Third, the money would have to reach the same families. Programmes are built for the poorest households, and the poorest households are the ones least likely to have a relative abroad. If none of the three holds, the assumption behind the aid cuts fails, and small islands are the places where it fails first. Hold on to these three, because the three islands are going to test them one at a time. Quick check before I go on: which of the three surprises you most? Just call it out.

Now I want your answer. Think of a family on your island. A daughter or a son abroad has been paying the school fees for years. Count each one out loud, then ask one person from the Caribbean and one from the Pacific why, thirty seconds each. Then press R. Here is why the answer is not obvious. In 2009, when the world economy fell, remittances fell and aid rose on all three islands: in Tonga, family money went from 29 to 24 per cent of GDP while aid went from 7 to 11 per cent of national income. A hurricane in Jamaica or the eruption in Tonga is different: one island was hit and the other two were not. In that year the family abroad and the donor both reacted to the same event, so the model can compare the two reactions on the same island, in the same year, and see which flow rose, by how much, and how quickly. The hypothesis

What a transfer is attached to decides what it does inside a household

What happens to a remittance when an aid programme begins paying for something the sender was paying for?

If the transfer is attached to people, the sender keeps sending and the household puts the money on another need. If it is attached to an item, the sender reduces or stops. We predict that remittances behave in the first way and that programme aid behaves in the second.

The survey tests three claimsWho pays for whatWhether a stated purpose bindsWhat happens when the payer changes

Here is what the project predicts, and the idea behind it. Money that arrives from abroad is attached to something, and what it is attached to decides what happens when a programme takes over. Look at the left panel. The daughter sends money for the school fee. When a programme starts paying the fee, the reason for her transfer is gone, so she sends less or stops. Now the right panel. The daughter sends money so the household is supported. When the programme pays the fee, she keeps sending and the family puts the money on the roof instead. The hypothesis is in the frame above: we predict that family money behaves like the right panel, because it is attached to people, and that programme money behaves like the left panel, because it is attached to an item. For thirty years research on public money and private transfers has assumed the left panel for both, from national totals, without asking a household. The survey tests the three claims at the bottom of the screen: who pays for what in a household, whether a purpose stated by the sender actually binds, and what happens the last time the payer changed. This is how the project asks the households themselves, in each country, in this order. First, interviews with ten to twenty key informants chosen with the local partners: the village authority, the church or mosque leader, the transfer agent, a returned seasonal worker. Every one of them gets the same five questions, starting with: when a family here gets money from someone abroad, what happens to it? Second, three focus groups. The group lists the things a family here has to find money for, one card each, then spreads a hundred beans across the cards to show what family abroad pays for, then does it again for work here, for programmes, for the church or mosque. Then we move the beans. If a programme started paying the school fee, what does the sister abroad do with her money? Whether the beans move to another card or leave the ground is the whole study, and a group can show it in twenty minutes. Third, the household survey: 250 to 300 families in each country, in six communities of about fifty, built around a table of thirteen things a household pays for, asking who paid for each one in the last year and what happened the last time the payer changed. Behind the fieldwork sit the three country models I mentioned at the start, one per island on that island's own yearly record, 44 years for Jamaica, 31 for Tonga and 22 for Comoros, each asking whether the two flows replace each other, add to each other or leave each other alone once the world shock is removed. Jamaica from April 2027, Tonga in July and August, Comoros in September and October. Whatever we find goes back to the communities that produced it. The survey is built so that the answer to the question you gave me a few minutes ago comes from families, one household at a time, rather than from national totals. One last question for you, and it is in the focus groups too. A programme repairs your water tank, or a relative sends the same amount in cash. Which would a family on your island rather have? Hands for the tank. Hands for the cash.
