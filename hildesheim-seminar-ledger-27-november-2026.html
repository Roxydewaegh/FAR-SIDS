Good afternoon, and thank you, Yves, for the invitation. My name is Roxane de Waegh. I am Belgian, I did my doctorate in New Zealand on small islands in the Pacific, and I now work at the Islands and Small States Institute of the University of Malta on a Marie Skłodowska-Curie fellowship that studies how households in small island states live on money from outside, sent by relatives abroad or paid through foreign aid, in Jamaica, Tonga and the Comoros.

Your course asks how small states experienced the twentieth century, what opportunities and challenges they met, and which factors played a role. I answer with one European small state, Malta, where I live, in five stages, and then set Jamaica beside it. The three cards show the plan. In the last part of the session we turn the same question to Germany, because most of you will soon teach this history in German schools.

The question for today is not whether small states were dependent. Malta shows how dependent they could be: in 1957 the British naval dockyard employed about 12,000 people, on an island whose census that year counted 319,620 people. My question is how much agency a small island’s government and its families had, and by agency I mean something modest and precise, shown in the yellow box: how far they could make their own decisions when a larger power set most of the terms.

Two kinds of money from outside run through the whole seminar. The first is remittances, the money emigrants send home to their families. The middle card gives the first year for which the World Bank has figures for both islands, 1976: remittances were worth 6.1% of Malta’s GDP and 3.0% of Jamaica’s, so at that point they mattered more to Malta. GDP, gross domestic product, is the value of everything a country produces in a year. The second kind is aid, money one government gives another. For Malta after independence in 1964 it came as British and NATO payments that the Maltese government negotiated. We will follow how the weight of these two kinds of money changed in each island, and who made the decisions about them.

Stage one. For the first half of the century the British navy was Malta’s main employer. The drawing stands for the naval dockyard in the Grand Harbour, which in 1957 had about 12,000 workers, on an island where the census that year counted 319,620 people. During the Second World War the island was besieged from 1940 until May 1943, and on 15 April 1942 King George VI awarded the whole island the George Cross.

Read the timeline on the right from the top. In April 1957 the British government published its Defence White Paper, which planned smaller armed forces overseas after the Suez crisis. Britain’s own Colonial Secretary, Alan Lennox-Boyd, recognised what that meant: if the dockyard were closed or substantially run down, he wrote, Malta faced economic ruin. That decision was taken in London.

The red point is Malta’s reply, and it matters because the Maltese government was not passive. The dockyard was also a political question for the prime minister, Dom Mintoff, because, as Simon Smith notes, its 12,000 workers were his main base of support. In December 1957 Mintoff offered his resignation, and instead of leaving he introduced a motion in the legislative assembly declaring that the Maltese people were no longer bound by their agreements and obligations to Britain unless Britain guaranteed that employment would not fall. So the decision to cut came from London, and the Maltese government answered with the one pressure it had: the threat to withdraw its cooperation.

This stage gives us the first two conditions for the list we build today, shown at the bottom: a home market too small to employ its people, and dependence on one larger country.

Stage two. Malta’s government answered a population it could not employ by helping people to leave. Russell King, working from the records of Malta’s Department of Emigration, reports that since 1946 over 140,000 Maltese emigrated through the assisted passage scheme. Under that scheme the government paid most of the fare and the emigrant paid, in King’s words, only a token contribution to the air or boat fares. The scheme began under colonial rule and continued after independence in 1964.

The chart shows where they went, from King’s table for 1946 to 1976: 57.6% to Australia, which is 80,952 people, 22% to Britain, 13% to Canada and 7% to the United States. The bars show shares of all assisted emigrants over those thirty years, not people who went in any one year. The census of 1948 counted 305,991 people in Malta, so the emigrants of these thirty years equalled more than two in five of that population, and King calls Malta the European country with the highest rate of emigration after the war.

Two decisions sit side by side here. The government decided to pay the fares, and that decision appears in the records with its budget line. Families decided who would go and how much money to send home, and those decisions appear almost nowhere. The money did come back: on the World Bank series, money sent home was worth 6.9% of Malta’s GDP in 1971, the first year the series covers. That figure counts only transfers that passed through banks, not the postal orders, parcels and cash that emigrants carried home on visits. Keep these family decisions in mind, because the debate at the end of the session returns to them.

Stage three is the one your students will not expect. In February 1956 Malta voted in a referendum on joining the United Kingdom, with Maltese members in the House of Commons. On the first card are the figures the Colonial Secretary gave the House of Commons in March 1956: about 67,000 voted yes and about 20,000 voted no, so three in four of those who voted said yes. But only about 90,000 of the 153,000 people on the register took part, because the Nationalist Party told its supporters to boycott the vote, and the Church also opposed integration. Smith calculates that only 44.24% of the whole electorate voted yes, and the British government did not go ahead.

The second card explains why. Mintoff would only join Britain on what he called equivalence, and in talks in February 1957 he spelled it out: within ten years, health, education and social services comparable to Britain’s, and equal wages for government employees. The Treasury estimated the cost of extending British social services and spending levels to Malta at £10 million a year. Smith concludes that Mintoff’s determination to achieve equivalence clashed with the British government’s concern to limit its financial liabilities towards Malta.

The third card is the outcome. After the dockyard dispute of stage one, Mintoff announced his resignation in April 1958, the governor was allowed to declare a state of emergency on 29 April 1958, and Britain governed Malta directly for more than three years. Malta became independent on 21 September 1964, with a ten year defence treaty under which Britain kept its base and committed itself, in Dominic Fenech’s words, to modest loans and grants.

Put the two questions at the bottom to the room. The first gives us the third condition: does the state hold something a larger country needs, and can its government make that country pay for it? Malta held the harbour. In 1956 its government could not turn that into British money on its terms. In 1972 it could, which is stage four. The second question is for you as teachers: in this case a colony voted to stay and the empire said no. Ask what that does to the independence story your pupils learn.

Stage four is the one moment in the century when Malta’s government set the terms, and it is our clearest case of agency. Mintoff returned to office in June 1971 and told Britain that it would now pay rent for its bases on the island. Henry Kissinger’s memorandum to President Nixon of 11 March 1972 records where the talks stood: the central question remained money, Mintoff wanted a one time payment of five million pounds on top of the 14 million pound offer from Britain and NATO, and the allies were holding firm. The agreement was signed in London on 26 March 1972 for seven years, and a State Department memorandum of December 1972 describes it as giving Malta 14 million pounds a year in rent. Dominic Fenech writes that the new agreement restricted Britain’s use of the base and more than tripled the rent. Simon Smith, who has read the British files for 1964 to 1971, describes the Maltese as adept at manipulating, cajoling and even threatening the former imperial power to keep and increase its commitments.

The chart shows net official aid to Malta as a share of national income, from the World Bank. The line is already high in 1970, at 7.2%, because Britain was still paying the loans and grants agreed at independence. It dips to 4.7% in 1972, the year of the negotiation, and rises to 7.5% in 1973, the first full year of the new agreement. It then falls to 5.0% by 1979 and to 1.2% in 1980, after the base closed. The chart measures official aid only, so it shows the size and timing of Malta’s outside public money rather than every payment under the agreement, and the rises and falls after 1980 are too small to read much into.

The agreement had an end date. In 1979 the British base closed peacefully and, as Fenech puts it, all occupied lands were returned to the Maltese government. Maltese governments then built a new economy through tourism, manufacturing and, from 1 May 2004, membership of the European Union. So the third condition has two parts: a small state needs something a larger country wants, and its government needs to be able to make that country pay for it, which Malta’s government did for seven years.

Stage five is about sources, where a historian’s training and my own work meet. On the left are the records governments kept. Smith wrote his article from British Cabinet and Colonial Office files. Kissinger’s memorandum on the rent sits in the American archive. The 1972 agreement is a signed text with a price and an end date. Every decision we have followed so far left paper of this kind.

On the right is what survives about families. King worked from the records of Malta’s Department of Emigration: the forms emigrants filled in before leaving, which give name, sex, date of birth, marital status, occupation, address and destination, and, from 1 January 1976, cards for returning migrants with the date of return. These records tell a historian who left, when and where to, but not why, who in the family decided, or what the money sent home paid for. Letters and postal orders survive only when a family kept them. For money sent home today, a central bank records the total that arrives through banks and transfer companies, which is where the World Bank figures come from, but not who decided or what it paid for.

So a history of Malta’s dependency written from what survives is mostly a history of what the British and Maltese governments decided. That is not a criticism of Smith, who reads the files very well; it is a limit of the files. Decolonising that history, in the sense I use the word in my own work, means asking the people the decisions reached and treating what they say as evidence of the same standing as a minute written in Whitehall. Put the question on the screen to the room before moving on. This is also why my own project asks Jamaican households directly, which brings us to Jamaica.

After the war Jamaica followed the same route as Malta. From 1948 people from the Caribbean, Jamaicans among them, moved to Britain to work. The Commonwealth Immigrants Act, passed in April 1962, brought immigration control into force on 1 July 1962, five weeks before Jamaican independence on 6 August.

Canada changed its immigration regulations in 1962, removing most of the open racial criteria and making skills the main test, and the United States changed its immigration law in 1965. According to the Organization of American States’ migration profile for Jamaica, both countries then admitted migrants on occupational and educational criteria, and Jamaicans turned there instead.

The chart shows the result a decade later, from the Migration Policy Institute: in the 1970s, 256,984 Jamaicans emigrated to the United States, 56,964 to Canada and 13,831 to Britain. The bars count people who left during those ten years, not the number of Jamaicans already living in each country. In 2010, when the Bank of Jamaica surveyed people who receive money from abroad, 62% of them received it from the United States, and the United Kingdom and Canada followed well behind.

The chart shows the money emigrants sent home as a share of GDP for both islands, from the same World Bank series and for the same years. Start on the left. In 1976, the first year with figures for both islands, remittances were worth 6.1% of Malta’s GDP and 3.0% of Jamaica’s, so they mattered more to Malta. After the British base closed in 1979, Maltese governments built new sources of income through tourism, manufacturing and, from 2004, membership of the European Union. As the Maltese economy grew, the money emigrants sent home became a small part of it: 0.5% of GDP by 1999. Jamaica’s economy did not create enough work for its people, so Jamaicans kept leaving, and the money they sent home rose to 8.7% of GDP by 1999 and to 16% by 2024.

Two features of the chart need explaining, because a careful student will ask about them. First, the dashed lines. Malta’s line jumps from 0.5% in 2003 to 5.2% in 2004, the year Malta joined the European Union, and falls from 2.1% in 2016 to 0.2% in 2017. Nothing happened to Maltese families in those single years to explain jumps of that size, so the likeliest explanation is a change in how Malta records these transfers; I have not found a published explanation, so I say that plainly and read Malta’s line only up to 2003. Second, Jamaica’s peak of 23% in 2021. Jamaica’s GDP fell by 8.3% in 2020, during the pandemic, while the money sent home rose from 2.6 billion US dollars in 2019 to 3.1 billion in 2020 and 3.7 billion in 2021. The share jumped because the money rose while the economy shrank, and it fell back to 16% by 2024 as the economy recovered, although the amount stayed close to 3.6 billion dollars.

What the two lines mean for agency. The Jamaican government still negotiates a great deal of the island’s income from abroad: tourism, bauxite, loans and aid. What changed is that a large share of that income now also arrives as private transfers to households, and the government does not decide how those transfers are spent. In Malta, most income from abroad after 1979 came through tourism, exports and European membership, which governments could plan for and shape.

Now the question on the screen. At first most people say that money should go straight to families: it arrives quickly, nobody takes a share on the way, and the family decides what it needs. Then look at the two figures on the screen. Jamaica’s Survey of Living Conditions for 2008, reported by the Bank of Jamaica, found that 43 households in 100 had received money from abroad in the previous twelve months, which means 57 in 100 had not. Money sent home reaches only the households that have someone abroad. The Bank of Jamaica’s own survey in 2010 found that people who receive money often use 85% of it for utility bills and basic living costs such as food and school, so for those households the money pays for everyday needs.

Now the argument for the government. Money a government receives can pay for a school, a road or a clinic that every household uses, including the 57 in 100 with nobody abroad, and some of those households may be among the poorest. A government can also plan with money it receives, which it cannot do with money that arrives in private hands. Use the three prompts: who decides how it is spent, who can plan with it, and who gets nothing. Let the room argue for three minutes. There is no single right answer, and both positions have serious defenders.

For the last part of the session we take the question from Malta and Jamaica and turn it to a country you know well. The line across the screen shows the agreements. On 20 December 1955 the West German labour minister Anton Storch and the Italian foreign minister Gaetano Martino signed the first recruitment agreement, in Rome. West Germany then signed with Spain and Greece in 1960, Turkey in 1961, Morocco in 1963, Portugal in 1964, Tunisia in 1965 and Yugoslavia in 1968, eight agreements in all. According to the Federal Agency for Civic Education, about 14 million people came to work in West Germany up to 1973, and just over 11 million of them went home again. On 23 November 1973 the federal labour minister, Walter Arendt, stopped recruitment, giving as his reason that the energy crisis might harm employment in the coming months.

The arrangement has the same parts as the story of Malta and Jamaica: a larger country that needs workers, other countries that send them, and families who decide who goes and what money comes home. So the question is the same one, asked of Germany: who benefited more, the receiving country or the sending countries, and who decided the terms? You will work on it in three groups, each from one side.

The fact sheets you received carry the same facts.

Group one takes West Germany’s side. During the economic boom employers needed workers, and recruiting from structurally weaker regions abroad added workers without driving up wages. On 23 November 1973 the federal government stopped recruitment on its own decision. Answers I expect: West Germany gained labour and growth, and controlled when recruitment started and stopped; the plan that most workers would go home did not hold, because many stayed.

Group two takes the side of the sending governments, with Turkey as the example. Turkey had high population growth and serious underemployment, and it wanted foreign currency. Between 1961 and 1973 about 867,000 Turkish workers came to West Germany, and about 500,000 of them returned. In 1972 Turkish workers sent home 2.1 billion Deutschmarks, more than Turkey’s trade deficit that year of 1.8 billion Deutschmarks. Answers I expect: Turkey sent away part of its unemployment and earned foreign currency, but its government could not prevent the 1973 stop, and it lost many of its younger workers.

Group three takes the families’ side. After the 1973 stop, many workers who were already in West Germany stayed and brought their families to join them. Between 1974 and 1979 the proportion of women among people from Turkey in West Germany grew by about 21%, and the number of children under 15 doubled to about 420,000. Answers I expect: families gained wages and the money sent home, and paid for them with years of separation; after 1973 many decided to settle rather than return, which was a decision of their own, taken within terms that Bonn had set.

The three prompts push the debate further. The first asks what counts as a benefit: a state’s accounts show growth and foreign currency, while a family’s life shows wages, separation and, for many, a new home. The second asks about power: the West German government signed the agreements and ended recruitment in 1973 on its own decision, while the sending governments signed but could not stop anything. The third turns the question around: after 1973, families decided whether to stay and whether to bring their children, and those decisions changed Germany more than the agreements themselves had planned. Draw out the pattern we saw in Malta: the larger power set the terms, and governments and families made their own decisions within them.

Now show the answers, after the room has offered its own. The squares compare scale. Each grid has 100 squares. More than 140,000 Maltese emigrated between 1946 and 1976, equal to 45.8% of the 305,991 people counted in the 1948 census, so nearly half the squares are filled. About 867,000 Turkish workers came to West Germany between 1961 and 1973, equal to 2.4% of Turkey’s population of 36.6 million in 1970, and about 500,000 of them returned, so only two squares and a fraction are filled. The periods and the measures differ, so read the grids as an order of size rather than an exact comparison.

The three cards on the right give the reasons I would draw out. The first is scale: a small island that loses that share of its people changes in a way a large country does not. The second is partners: Malta depended on one larger country, Britain, for work and later for the rent on its harbour. The third is what happens when recruitment ends: a small economy has fewer jobs to take back the people who return; Malta’s government, for example, had to build new industries after the British base closed in 1979.

The students may add reasons of their own, such as identity, the politics of a diaspora, or the size of government, and that is welcome. For those who want to read further, the box under the three cards names two traditions. Bertram and Watters described the smallest island economies as resting on migration, remittances, aid and government employment, and read that as dependence. Katzenstein, on Europe’s small states, and Baldacchino, on islands, read the same kind of history and find bargaining, which fits Malta’s own choices in 1957 and 1972.

Close the debate with the question on the screen. Some of your future pupils may be grandchildren of people who came to West Germany under the recruitment agreements signed between 1955 and 1968, and others will have no family link to them at all. How would you teach this history?

Offer the three ideas one at a time. The first is a family interview as homework: pupils ask a relative or a neighbour about work and migration, and then present what they found to the class. Remind the students that this homework must always be optional and that no pupil should ever have to share a family history. Pupils without a family link, or who prefer not to use it, take the second or the third idea.

The second idea needs no family link and no archive. Pupils walk their own streets and list the shops, restaurants, clubs and places of worship that recruited workers and their families opened. They then find out when each one opened and who decided to open it, which usually leads back to a family that chose to stay after 1973. The third idea sends pupils to their town’s records, such as the local newspaper or the history of a large employer, to find recruited workers and ask whose decisions those sources show. That question brings back stage five on whose decisions get written down. If a town has no such records, the street survey still works.

The fourth line is left open. Ask the room for a fourth idea of their own, and what they would change in the first three for their own classes. Take two or three answers; there is no need to reach a conclusion.
