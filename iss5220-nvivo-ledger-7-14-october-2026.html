Session one · The standard for a qualitative study

A reader can trust a qualitative finding only if every step from the text to the themes can be checked

A path the reader can check

What a good methods section shows

A codebook in which every code is named, with a definition and an example passage; a record of which sources each code was applied to; memos saying why codes were merged or split; and a count of the sources behind each theme.

A path the reader cannot check

What examiners reject

Themes announced in the findings with no codebook, no counts and no record of decisions, so that the reader has to take the researcher's word for them. Examiners and reviewers ask for the codebook first.

Qualitative analysisWorking with words rather than numbers: interview transcripts, field notes, documents or published papers. The analysis is how you move from many pages of text to a few themes.
A themeA pattern of meaning that recurs across the text, for example that obligations are paid before everyday spending. A qualitative study usually reports a small number of themes and what they mean.
A codeA short label you write on a passage of text to say what it is about. Codes are the smallest unit of the analysis, and themes are built from them.
A codebookThe list of all your codes, each with a one line definition and an example passage. Examiners ask for it first.

Let me start with a question for the room. How many of you will use interviews, focus groups or documents in your dissertation? Nearly all of you, and that means nearly all of you will be marked against the standard on this screen. So let me explain what qualitative analysis is, in plain words. Qualitative analysis means working with words rather than numbers. The words might be interview transcripts, field notes, documents, or, in a review like mine, hundreds of published papers. You start with many pages of text, and you end with a small number of themes and a claim about what they mean.

A theme is a pattern of meaning that comes back again and again across the text. For example, in the Samoa paper you will read today, one pattern is that families pay their customary obligations before they pay for everyday things. A code is much smaller than a theme. A code is a short label you write on one passage to say what that passage is about. Codes are the smallest unit of the analysis, and themes are built from them. A codebook is simply the list of all your codes, each with a one line definition and an example passage.

Now look at the two paths on the screen. On the top path, a reader can check every step: which passages were read, what label each one was given, how the labels were grouped into themes, and who decided at each step. On the bottom path, the themes appear in the findings chapter and nothing sits behind them. The reader has to take the researcher's word for it. Examiners reject the bottom path, and they reject it by asking one question: can I see your codebook?

Braun and Clarke are the two psychologists whose method we use. In their 2006 paper they say that the most common weakness in published qualitative work is exactly this hidden path from codes to themes. So everything NVivo will do for you next week produces one of the four records in the left card: the codebook, the record of which sources each code was applied to, the memos, and the counts. Keep those four in mind, because they are the reason we learn the software at all.

Before you write a single code, you need a question. This sounds obvious, and it is the step most students skip. Without a question, coding drifts into describing everything on the page, and you end up with a hundred labels that say what the text is about and nothing about what you wanted to know. The question is what decides three things: which passages count, which codes earn a place in the codebook, and which patterns are big enough to call a theme. Braun and Clarke put it in one line: a theme captures something important in relation to the research question.

Here is how it worked in the review. Our question was: what do the published studies say about how aid and remittances support wellbeing in small island states? Every one of the 760 labels was written against that question. When a paper spent two pages on the history of a country's constitution, I read those pages and wrote nothing on them, because they said nothing to the question, and leaving them unlabelled was the question doing its job.

Thematic analysisA method for finding patterns of meaning in a body of text by coding it and grouping the codes. Braun and Clarke (2006) set out the six phases, and their 2019 paper says the phases are not a fixed recipe, because the researcher's judgement drives the analysis.
Where the software helpsNVivo helps most in phase 2, where it keeps every label and passage findable, and it makes phase 4 a single click. Phases 3 and 5 depend on your judgement, whatever tool you use.

The method is called thematic analysis, and Braun and Clarke set it out in 2006 as six phases. Let me walk you through them in plain words, because the names in the paper are more formal than the work is. In phase one you read everything once, with no pen in your hand, so that you know the whole of your material before you start cutting it up. In phase two you code, which means you give each passage that says something to your question a short label. In phase three you gather the codes into candidate themes, which are groups of codes that share a subject. In phase four you check each candidate theme against the text: you go back to the passages under it and ask whether every one of them belongs there. In phase five you define and name each theme in a paragraph that says what it covers and what it leaves out. And in phase six you write up, with the studies or the interviews behind each theme counted and named.

The review followed exactly these phases, twice over. In phases one and two, at the screening stage, I read the 282 studies that met our rules and wrote a short label for each theme in an abstract, which came to 760 different labels. In phases three to five, those labels were gathered into 43 sub-themes under ten themes, and each label was filed under one theme and one sub-theme. Then the 201 papers that were finally included were read in full, and the map was rebuilt from that reading as 46 themes arranged in four layers. In phase six, each theme is reported with the number of papers behind it and with those papers named. So when you see a theme in the paper we publish, there are labels and papers behind it, and a reader can follow the trail back.

One warning that Braun and Clarke themselves give in their 2019 paper. Students often treat the six phases as a checklist, six boxes to tick in order. They are not. You will go back from phase four to phase two when a code turns out to cover two different things, and going back is the method working as it should. Your analysis is defensible when you wrote down what you decided at each phase, so that someone else can see it, whatever order you did them in.

Where does the software come in? NVivo helps most in phase two, because it keeps every label and every passage findable, which a pile of printed pages does not. And it turns phase four into a single click: you open a code and every passage under it appears in front of you. Phases three and five, the grouping and the naming, depend on your thinking, whatever tool you use. No software does those for you, and next week you will see exactly where the software stops.

Session one · A decision to make before you code

Your codes come from the text or from a list you made first, and your write up says which

From the text

Braun and Clarke call this inductive

You read a passage and write a label for what it is about. The review worked this way: "church giving" exists as a label because papers kept describing money given to churches, not because anyone listed it in advance.

From a list you made first

Braun and Clarke call this deductive, or theoretical

You start from three or four codes your question makes you expect, and you apply them as you read, adding a code when a passage fits none of them. Faster, and it can miss what you did not expect.

For the classwork

Either way is acceptable. Your 400 words state which one you used, and if you started from the review's codes on stop 7, you say so.

Braun, V. and Clarke, V. (2006) Using thematic analysis in psychology. Qualitative Research in Psychology 3(2), 77 to 101, pages 83 to 84, on inductive and theoretical thematic analysis.

There is one decision to make before you start coding, and your write up has to say what you decided. Where do your codes come from? There are two answers, and both are fine, as long as you say which one you took.

The first answer is that the codes come from the text. You read a passage, you ask what it is about, and you write that down as a label. You did not know the label existed until you read the passage. The review worked this way. The label "church giving" is in our codebook because paper after paper described money given to churches, not because anyone sat down beforehand and decided churches would matter. Braun and Clarke call this inductive coding, and you can use that word in your write up, but the plain description is: from the text.

The second answer is that the codes come from a list you made first. You look at your question, you write down the three or four codes it makes you expect, and you read the paper looking for them. When a passage fits one of your codes, you apply it. When a passage fits none of them and still says something to your question, you add a new code. Braun and Clarke call this deductive, or theoretical, coding. It is faster, and it has one risk: you can miss the thing you did not expect, because you were only looking for what was on your list.

For the classwork, either way is acceptable. On 14 October some of you will start from the review's codes, which you will see on stop 7, and that is a list made first. Some of you will read your paper cold and label as you go. Your 400 words say which one you did, and examiners will respect that plain statement more than any amount of theory.

Let me trace one example through the review, so that you can see the three levels with real names on them. The label is the wording written on a passage. "Foreign aid" was written on 48 papers. At the other end, "Women in Development aid-funded credit schemes" was written on one paper. Between those two there were 760 distinct labels across the 282 abstracts read at screening.

The sub-theme is the group that labels were gathered into. "Foreign aid", "ODA", "aid allocation" and 25 other labels became one sub-theme called "Aid flows and programmes", and that sub-theme was found in 160 of the studies. Then the theme is the group that sub-themes belong to. "Aid flows and programmes" is one of four sub-themes under the theme "Aid and donor politics", and that theme is one of ten themes in the July mapping.

In NVivo those three levels are three things you can point to on the screen. A label is a code. A sub-theme is a parent code with the labels as its child codes underneath it. And the count of studies behind a theme comes from a query, which is a question you ask the software about your coding. I did these steps in spreadsheets, and the software does the same three steps, which is exactly why I teach the method first and the software second. If you understand the three levels, the buttons are easy. If you only know the buttons, you do not know what you are building.

Defensible means this. A reader who doubts that "Kinship, reciprocity and custom" is a real theme can open the 33 studies behind it and read the passages that were coded. The reader does not have to agree with me, but the reader can see what was done, which is the standard we set on stop 2.

Session one · The mistake examiners see most often

A code that repeats the paper's headings has sorted the paper, not analysed it

What the mistake looks like

Codes that match the section headings, or the interview questions

A student codes the three pages you just read to one code called "Uses of remittances", because that is the paper's heading. Every sentence goes under it. Nothing has been decided, so nothing can be compared.

What the review did instead

Seven labels on the same three pages

Customary obligation (17 in every 100 spent before the tsunami, 9 after), church giving (12, then 9), food (13, then 16), housing (13, then 22.5 for rebuilding), health (13 after), business investment (3, then 1.5), and the passage on page 663 where remittances rose because aid arrived late, labelled "remittance as social insurance mechanism".

Themes do not emerge

You decide them, and you write that down

Braun and Clarke ask you never to write "themes emerged from the data". The passive hides the person who did the work. Write "I grouped these codes into this theme because", and the memo on stop 20 is where that sentence lives.

Before we go to the software, one mistake, because it is the one examiners see most often and the one NVivo makes easiest to commit. Braun and Clarke list it among their pitfalls: using the interview questions, or the section headings of a document, as your codes. Here is what it looks like with the pages you just coded. The paper's heading for those pages is "4.4 What are remittances used for?". A student makes one code called "Uses of remittances" and puts all three pages under it. It feels like coding, because text is highlighted and a code has a count. But nothing has been decided. The student has sorted the paper by its own headings, and the reader learns nothing that the table of contents did not already say.

Look at what the review did with the same three pages, where it wrote seven labels. Customary obligation, which in the paper is fa'alavelave, the weddings, funerals and title ceremonies: 17 in every 100 spent before the tsunami, and 9 after. Church giving: 12 before, 9 after. Food: 13 before, 16 after. Housing: 13 before, and 22.5 after, because people were rebuilding. Health care appears after the tsunami at 13. Business investment: 3 before, 1.5 after. And on page 663, a passage where remittances rose because government and NGO aid arrived late. The review gave that its own label, "remittance as social insurance mechanism", in the paper's own words, because it speaks to our question about how the two kinds of money meet.

Each of those labels answers the question, and each can be counted across other papers. A code called "Uses of remittances" can be counted too, but the count means nothing, because everything is in it. That is the test for your own codes: could a reader compare this code across two papers and learn something? If the code is the paper's heading, the answer is no.

One more thing from Braun and Clarke, and it is about a single word. Please never write "themes emerged from the data". Themes do not emerge. You decided them, and the passive voice hides the person who did the work, which is the opposite of what stop 2 asks for. Write "I grouped these codes into this theme because", and then give the reason. Next week you will see where that sentence lives in NVivo: it is a memo, and it is on stop 20.

Session two · Reading a code

Opening a code shows every passage coded to it, which is phase four of thematic analysis

  1. In the Codes list, double click a code. The detail view shows every passage coded to it, from every file, with the file name beside each passage.
  2. Read the two counts in the Codes list. Files is the number of sources the code was applied to, and References is the number of passages.
  3. Read the passages together and ask two questions. Do these passages belong together? Is this code different from every other code? If some passages do not belong, uncode them. If the code covers two different things, split it into two child codes and write the reason in a memo.
  4. Keep the passage that does not fit the pattern. Give it a code of its own rather than deleting it. A finding that contradicts the others is reported, not hidden.

Now read the passages together, and this is phase four of Braun and Clarke, checking the theme against the text. Ask two questions. First: do these passages belong together? Read them one after another and see whether they are about the same thing. Second: is this code different from every other code? Session three · Crosstab query

The crosstab query counts coded passages by attribute, which turns several coded sources into a comparison

  1. On the Explore tab, choose Queries, then Crosstab. Drag your codes from the list view into the Codes panel, or click the plus sign and tick them.
  2. Under "show code against", choose Attributes. Choose the classification Paper and the attribute Country. Each value of Country becomes a column.
  3. Click Run Query. Each cell gives the number of passages coded to that code in the papers with that country. On the Results tab you can switch the cells to coding presence, which shows only whether a code appears at all.
  4. With one paper the table has one column and shows nothing new. With the twenty interviews of a dissertation and their attributes, the crosstab is the analysis. The matrix coding query on the same menu builds the same table by hand, one item at a time.

Session three · The limits of the software

NVivo stores, retrieves and counts coded text, and the researcher interprets it

A count is not a finding

Forty references

Forty references under a code mean that you coded forty passages. The count does not say what the passages mean, whether you applied the code the same way each time, or whether the one passage that says the opposite is among them.

A query returns only what you coded

The coding comes first

The coding query cannot find a passage you did not code, so every result depends on the coding, and the coding depends on the codebook.

The software does not decide

The researcher decides

Which passages matter, what to call them and how to group them are your decisions. NVivo records those decisions so that a reader can check them.

How a dissertation reports it

"Twelve of the twenty interviews were coded to obligation, and in eight of those the passage was also coded to disaster recovery (coding query, all of these codes, run on 14 October 2026). The codebook in Appendix A gives the definition and an example passage for each code."

At this point somebody always asks whether the software does the analysis for them. It does not, and I want to be exact about where it stops. NVivo stores coded text, retrieves it and counts it. It does not interpret it. Forty references under a code mean that you coded forty passages. The count does not say what those passages mean, whether you applied the code the same way each time, or whether the one passage that says the opposite of the other thirty nine is among them. That passage is the one an examiner will ask about.

A query returns only what you coded. The coding query cannot find a passage you did not code, and the crosstab cannot compare cases you did not classify. So every result depends on the coding, and the coding depends on the codebook, and the codebook depends on the definitions you wrote on stop 15. The chain runs backwards to a sentence you typed.

And the software does not decide. Which passages matter, what to call them, how to group them into themes, and what the pattern means: those are your decisions. NVivo records them so that a reader can check them, which is the standard we started with on stop 2. The box on the screen shows how a dissertation reports this work, and every part of it came from a stop this afternoon. Twelve of the twenty interviews were coded to obligation. In eight of those the passage was also coded to disaster recovery, from a coding query with all of these codes, run on 14 October 2026. The codebook in Appendix A gives the definition and an example passage for each code. My review reports each theme the same way, with the number of studies behind it and the studies named, so that a reader can open them.
