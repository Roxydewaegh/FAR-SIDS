How do remittances and foreign aid shape wellbeing in island households?

FAR-SIDS is a two year study across Jamaica, Tonga, and Comoros, and the first to follow aid and remittances into the same households. It pairs econometric modelling with community led fieldwork and a household survey to understand dependency, agency, and self-reliance in Small Island Developing States.

The question

Why it matters

Small island developing states receive some of the world's largest flows of aid and remittances relative to their size. Almost every study looks at one flow or the other. No study anywhere has followed both into the same households.

FAR-SIDS asks which parts of a household's livelihood each flow pays for: food, school, medicine, the house, water and electricity, giving to the church or mosque, family and customary obligations, and the tools people use to earn a living. If the two sustain different parts, they cannot stand in for one another, and a cut in one is not made up by the other.

The project deliberately begins from island agency and self-determination rather than from vulnerability. Island communities are treated as the experts on their own lives and economies.

It studies three nations in three different oceans, each with a distinct history of migration, aid, and resilience, so that what is learned in one place can be read against the others.

The work is hosted at the Islands and Small States Institute at the University of Malta, itself a small island state, and runs with partner universities in each country studied.

The approach · the household survey

The household survey

The survey is the instrument that records both flows in a single household. Its questions were drawn from the three national household surveys these countries already run: the Jamaica Survey of Living Conditions, the Tonga Household Income and Expenditure Survey, and the Comoros EHCVM. Wherever those instruments cover the same ground, this one keeps their wording and their answer categories, so that the answers can be set beside national data. It is administered in person, under the same confidentiality rules as the national surveys.

At its centre is a payment matrix. For each of the things a household lives on it asks who paid, whether that was family abroad, a programme, the church or mosque, or the household itself. It then asks what happened the last time a payer changed.

Between 250 and 300 households answer in each country, a pilot decides the final length, and local research partners review the instrument before it is fielded. The instrument is public in the ledger below, module by module, in its three country forms, and the two registers list the programmes and schemes the survey asks about, under the names people use for them.

The approach · ethnographic fieldwork

Semi-structured interviews

The interviews are the first data collection in each country. They run in the opening weeks of the visit, before the household survey is fielded, and nothing in them depends on a household having answered the survey.

Ten to twenty key informants in each country, purposively selected for what their position lets them observe, with the local research partners deciding who fills each role. Households are not interviewed at this stage. Interviews are semi-structured, run sixty to ninety minutes, and are conducted with at least one local research partner in the language the participant prefers. In Tonga they are conducted as talanoa (Vaioleti, 2006).

Key informants

  • The village or district authority
  • The church or mosque leader
  • Staff of an implementing programme
  • The transfer agent or the shopkeeper who gives credit
  • A returned seasonal scheme worker
  • A statistics office contact
  • The national disaster office
  • The aid coordination unit
  • A savings group organiser
  • A builder or contractor
  • An elder or traditional leader
  • A women’s group leader

Five questions go to every key informant

  1. The pot“When a family here gets money from someone abroad, what happens to it? It arrives, and then, take me through it.”Establishes whether households keep money from different sources for different purposes, or pool it.
  2. What comes with it“Think of the last time someone you know received money from abroad. Did the person who sent it say what it was for?”Establishes whether a stated purpose is binding here, negotiable, or a courtesy.
  3. Where help comes from“Think of a family here that hit a hard month, the school fees due, or somebody sick. Where can they turn?”Builds the programme roster one name at a time, because it never asks anyone what foreign aid is.
  4. The change“Sometimes a programme, or the church, starts paying for something that a family used to get from relatives abroad. Have you seen that happen?”If yes, what happened next, and what the relative abroad did.
  5. What is enough, and the stop“Think of a family here that people say is doing alright. What does that family have?”Followed by its own question: if all the money and help from overseas stopped coming tomorrow, what would happen here?

Consent

Asking what a household receives, sends and spends is asking about private matters, and in a community where most people know each other an answer can travel. Consent is written and signed, recording happens by permission, and every transcript goes back to the participant for review before anything from it is used.

Next, the focus groups

The approach · ethnographic fieldwork

Focus groups

Three focus groups in each country, two split by gender and one mixed, with eight to ten participants in each. Participants are recruited through community leaders and local organisations, from communities like the ones the survey reaches. Nobody with authority over who receives a programme is invited, and nobody whose presence would stop others speaking; those people are heard separately as key informants. Each session runs about two and a half hours and has three parts.

Part one. The list the group makes, fifteen minutes

The facilitator asks one question: what are the things a family here has to find money for? Every answer goes on its own card as it is said, food, school, the church, a funeral, until the group runs out. Only then are the survey’s eleven categories laid on the ground beside the group’s cards, and the group is asked what we missed and what we got wrong.

Part two cannot run without this, because the beans have to be spread across a row of cards. Using the group’s own cards means the exercise measures their picture of household life rather than ours.

Part two. Where each source of money goes, fifty minutes

The group’s cards stay on the ground. The facilitator names one source of money at a time: family living abroad, work here, a programme, and the church or mosque. For each source in turn the group is handed one hundred beans and asked to place them on the cards, with more beans where that source pays for more. The same hundred beans are picked up and used again for the next source, so the group does four rounds of one hundred rather than four hundred at once.

The group argues until it agrees. The facilitator records the distribution and then asks why, because the explanation is worth more than the numbers. The count is always one hundred, in every group and in every country, so that one group can be set beside another. The method is proportional piling, from the ten-seed tradition of participatory appraisal, and it is used because one person can dominate a conversation but cannot dominate a hundred beans if the facilitator rotates who places them.

Then come the moves, which are the reason the focus groups exist. The facilitator points to a pile a programme pays for and asks what would happen if that programme stopped tomorrow, and who would pay instead. Then to a pile family abroad pay for, and asks what those relatives would do if a programme started paying for that thing: send the same amount, send less, or send the same amount for something else. The group moves the beans to show it. The third case is the group’s own choice, whichever change they think would hurt a family most.

Whether the beans move or leave is the distinction the whole study turns on, and a group can show it in twenty minutes.

Part three. Seven questions and no materials, forty minutes

The last part is a discussion, and two of its seven questions do most of the work.

Question three

The two houses

The facilitator describes two invented families of the same size. One has a well built house, a phone, a motorbike, and a daughter working abroad. The other has a simpler house, nobody abroad, and steady local work. Which family is doing better? The reasons the group gives test whether the things surveys usually measure miss what money from abroad actually builds.

Question four

The tank or the transfer

A programme repairs your water tank, or a relative sends you the same amount in cash. Which would a family here rather have, and why? The facilitator then changes the amounts and asks again, to see whether the preference holds when the money on offer is larger or smaller.

The other five ask what a family needs before people say it has enough, what would happen here if all the money and help from overseas stopped tomorrow, which months are hardest and what gets tried first when money runs short, whose work changes when one source takes over paying for something, and what nobody helps with at all.

What the interviews and the focus groups deliver before the survey is fielded

Whether households pool money or keep it separate

Do families here keep money from different places for different things, the school money staying school money, or does everything go into one pot and get spent on whatever comes up? The survey’s largest module assumes an answer to this, and nothing in the 201 studies reviewed has asked it.

Whether a stated purpose is binding

When someone abroad sends money, do they say what it is for? And where they do, does the household treat that as binding? We need to know whether he sent it for the roof describes a real arrangement, with expectations attached, or whether it is only a way of speaking.

Local wording for the survey

The local words for the survey’s key phrases: what people here call enough and living decently, the local names for the things households pay for, and the named events, the funerals, the misinale, the grand mariage.

The programme roster for each community

Every programme and service operating in each area, under the names people actually use, when each arrived, and what each asks of a household. The enumerator reads this list out, so that nobody is asked about programmes by names they have never heard.

Consent

Consent is written and signed, and the position on group confidentiality is said plainly at the start. At the end of the session the note-taker reads a summary back, the group corrects it there and then, and anything a participant wants removed comes out in the session. Sheets are photographed and people are not. Findings are shared back in an open community session in each country before the team leaves.

The approach · econometric models

Econometric models

The econometric side of the project asks whether aid and remittances have moved with wellbeing over four decades, one country at a time. Each country has one observation a year from 1980. The wellbeing indicators that reach that far back are life expectancy, infant and under-five mortality, and school enrolment.

Over a run that long almost every economic series drifts upward, so two unrelated series can appear to move together simply because both are rising. The models used here, called ARDL, were built for that problem and for series this short. They separate a long run relationship from a shared trend. They also allow an effect to arrive years after the money did, since remittances sent this year may show up in schooling or health much later.

Each country is modelled on its own, using World Bank and OECD data, rather than pooled into a regional average that would hide the differences between them. Before anything is estimated, each series is set against the years when something large happened, because a model that treats a hurricane year or a global recession as an ordinary year will read the recovery afterwards as a trend. The two pages below record those years.

Literature review · Reading the studies

How the forty-six themes were built

Every included study was read in full and entered as one row in a single table. The row records what the study measured, the method it used, the outcome it reported, how the money reached people, and where it disagrees with another study. Patterns were then coded across rows rather than within a single study. A pattern became a theme only where it appeared in more than one study and in more than one method. The list was checked against the table a second time, and four further readings were run without sight of it. The steps follow the six phases of thematic analysis set out by Braun and Clarke (2006).

The literature review · hypothesis and claims

What we argue, and why

The gap

Remittance research grew out of migration studies and household survey analysis, and it asks what households do with the money. Aid research grew out of public finance and the political economy of donor behaviour, and it asks what governments do with it. The two literatures therefore work at different units of analysis, and the project's review found no study that observes one household in receipt of both. Xenogiani (2006) named the interaction between the flows as an open question twenty years ago, and it is open on the same terms today.

What a transfer is attached to

Outside support is not all the same kind of money, because of the form it arrives in. The daughter in Auckland sends money for the school fee and says so, a brother in Marseille pays the electricity bill directly so the money never passes through the household at all, a programme grants a transfer on the condition that a child attends school, and a congregation expects the Misinale contribution. In the strongest cases the tie can be enforced, because the sender can ask how the money was used and can send less if the answer displeases her, and a programme can withhold a transfer when its condition is not met.

A transfer attached to an item exists so that a particular thing is paid for, and once another payer covers that thing the reason for the transfer is exhausted. A transfer attached to people exists so that a particular household is supported, and when one of that household's needs is covered elsewhere the transfer moves to another. A dollar is a dollar at a bank, and inside a household a roof is not a school fee. The distinction cannot be seen in any national total, but it can be observed directly in a single household.

The evidence that remittances arrive attached

  • About one third of remittance transactions to Tonga were at least partially earmarked for the Misinale.McKenzie, Gibson and Stillman, 2007
  • In a survey of Tongan migrants in New Zealand, the cash sent to a majority of recipients carried a special purpose.Gibson, McKenzie and Rohorua, 2006, study 082
  • Rural Samoan households in an ordinary year allocated about 17 per cent of remittance receipts to fa'alavelave, the ceremonial obligations, and about 12 per cent to the church.Le Dé, Gaillard, Friesen and Matautia Smith, 2015
  • Jamaican farming households reported that how the sender had indicated the funds should be used shaped what the remittance paid for, most strongly where sender and receiver were building a family house together.Thomas-Hope, 2017

Two shocks separate the item tie from the person tie

Tonga, July 2020

The borders closed and the church conferences, the Heilala festival and the Agricultural Show were all cancelled. Remittance receipts that month rose by 5.6 million pa'anga, 17.4 per cent, the highest monthly figure since 2014. Money tied to those events should have fallen with them.

Vainikolo, 2021
Samoa, after the 2009 tsunami

The fa'alavelave share of remittance spending fell from about 17 per cent to 9 and the church share from about 12 to 9, while food took 16 per cent and health care 13, because the same senders were meeting the emergency.

Le Dé and colleagues, 2015

In both cases the sums held or rose while their uses moved, which is what a tie to people produces and a tie to items cannot.

What happens to a remittance when an aid programme begins paying for something the sender was paying for?

If a transfer is attached to people, the sender continues to send when another payer takes over a need it was covering, and the household applies the money to a different need. If a transfer is attached to an item, the sender reduces or stops. We predict that remittances behave in the first way and that programme aid behaves in the second.

Three decades of crowding out research has assumed the opposite about private transfers, on national totals, without once testing it inside a household.

The three claims that make it testable

Claim 1

The map, who pays for what

What the survey records

For thirteen categories of household expenditure, from staple food to school fees, medicines, church giving and the tools of a living, the survey records which source was the principal payer over the preceding twelve months and which further sources contributed. No existing instrument asks that of both flows in one interview, and the map is a finding independent of every claim that follows it.

What would count against it

Most households cannot attribute expenditure by source and report a single pooled fund, an answer the enumerator offers on every row, so that the failure is reported by the project rather than discovered by a reviewer.

Claim 2

Attachment

What the survey records

Four properties establish that a tie exists and that it has force, and the survey asks them of remittances and of aid programme transfers in identical terms: whether senders state a purpose, whether anything is required of the household in return, whether any sender has established how a transfer was used, and whether any sender has reduced or stopped in consequence. The same four questions go to relatives living elsewhere in the country, so the three kinds of giver are compared on identical wording within one household.

What would count against it

Senders state a purpose but never establish how the money was used and never reduce what they send, because a purpose that carries no consequence is not a tie. It fails a second way if households report that everything received goes into one fund whatever it was sent for.

Claim 3

Reallocation, the payer change

What the survey records

Households report whether, over three years, anything on the list has changed payer or lost its payer, and for the most recent such change the survey records the earlier payer, the payer at interview, what the earlier payer did with what it used to give, and whether the new payer started before or after the earlier one changed. The same event is then approached from the other end, as a sender who stopped or reduced, together with the reason and the payer who succeeded, including the answer that nobody did.

The limits, fixed before fieldwork

An episode enters the test only where the household does not pool its money and where the new payer started before the earlier one changed. Households that keep everything in one fund answer the redirection question the same way whether or not anything moved, so the reading is confined to households that separate their money, and these counts are never turned into a rate for any country.

The claims are revised as the reading and the pilot sharpen the design, so this page states the argument rather than a fixed list.

This page belongs to the people the project works with. During fieldwork in Jamaica, Tonga, and Comoros, participants who choose to, and who give their consent, can share their stories here in whatever form they wish: photographs, artwork, video, or their own words. Over time it becomes a place where the islands publish their own account of aid, remittances and daily life. Nothing appears here until the fieldwork begins and the first participants choose to contribute.

Outreach · Field notebook

Field notebook, from 2027

This is a reflective journal of the research itself, recording what is being learned and rethought between Malta and the islands. It holds reflection rather than data, which makes it different from the timeline's logbook, where tasks are tracked. The first entries are written once the fieldwork begins.

Who

People and partners

Dr Roxane de Waegh

Marie Sklodowska-Curie Fellow · ISSI, University of Malta

Leads the research, from the econometric modelling to the fieldwork in each country. Her earlier work was carried out alongside island communities in several countries, and it decided how this project is run. Relationships are built before data is collected, and findings go back to the communities that produced them.
