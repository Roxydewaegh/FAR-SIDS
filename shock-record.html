FAR-SIDS shock ledger

The disaster years of Jamaica, Tonga and Comoros from 1980 to 2025, the rule that decides which of them enter the models as country shocks, and a plain explanation of the model they feed. Events are from EM-DAT (CRED / UCLouvain, public.emdat.be); population and GDP behind the thresholds are from the World Development Indicators.

    Each cell is one year, one row per decade. A coloured year is a year where S equals 1, in the colour of the hazard behind it; a year with two different hazards carries both colours. Hover or tab to a coloured year for the event and its figures.

    What S is and when it equals 1

    S is the country shock index: one value per country per year, 1 or 0. S equals 1 in a year where at least one natural hazard was large for the country it struck, and 0 in every other year. Large is decided by a written rule, applied the same way in all three countries, so that no event is added or dropped by judgement. An event clears the rule if it passes any one of three bars, each measured against the population and GDP of the year it happened.

    It reached many people

    At least 1 person in every 200 was affected, which is half of one per cent of that year's population.

    It killed at a national scale

    At least 1 death per 100,000 people, and never fewer than 5 deaths in total.

    It destroyed real wealth

    Reported damage worth at least 1 per cent of that year's GDP.

    Measuring against the year's own population and GDP is what makes the rule fair across three very different economies. A cyclone that reaches four thousand people is a national event in Tonga and a neighbourhood event in Jamaica, and the rule sees that difference.     The model needs S because a disaster year is not an ordinary year for either flow. Families abroad send more when the island is hit, and donors respond to the same event, so in those years both flows move for a reason that started outside the economy. S marks exactly those years, which lets the model account for them instead of mistaking a disaster response for the everyday relationship between aid and remittances.

    G is the opposite object: the years the whole world moved

    Aid and remittances both respond to the state of the world. In 2008 and 2009 the wages of migrants fell while emergency finance rose, and in 2020 and 2021 both flows rose together, so in those years every country's aid and remittance series moved at the same time whether or not the two flows had anything to do with each other. G marks that shared movement: it is world output growth, together with those two crisis episodes. The model removes what G explains, and only what survives that removal can count as a relationship between the two flows.

    World output growth, 1980 to 2025 (World Development Indicators). The shaded bands are 2008 to 2009 and 2020 to 2021.

    So S and G do opposite jobs and the models never mix them. A cyclone year moved one island and not the others, which is the closest thing to an experiment these series contain, so the model keeps S and reads it. A world crisis moved every island at once, which can only masquerade as a relationship, so the model removes G before believing anything. Keeping the two apart is a design decision, fixed before any estimate was run.

    The model, written out and explained

    The models run one country at a time, on annual data, and the three countries are never averaged together. Every letter below is one of the project's own data series, held under the same symbol. The equation is shown for the case where remittances are being explained, and the same equation is then run the other way round, with aid as the series being explained, because the question runs in both directions. Estimation does not start from this equation; it starts from a test, set out in step 0 below, that decides what the equation is allowed to mean.

    Rt  =  c  +  b · Rt−1  +  d₀ · At + d₁ · At−1  +  f₁ · Yt + f₂ · Pt + f₃ · Ot  +  s · St  +  g · Gt  +  et
    Rt, remittances this year

    The series being explained: the money families abroad sent to this country in year t. The little t means this year and t−1 means last year. R is the project's remittance series for this country, 44 usable years for Jamaica, 31 for Tonga, 23 for Comoros.

    b · Rt−1, last year's remittances

    Remittances this year look a lot like remittances last year, because the same families are sending for the same reasons. The number b measures that carry-over. Without this term, the model would credit aid with patterns that are really the series repeating itself. This is the autoregressive part of the model's name.

    d₀·At + d₁·At−1, the aid terms

    The question the model exists to answer. When aid this year, or aid last year, is higher than usual, do remittances sit higher or lower than their own history predicts? Aid enters with last year's value as well because families take time to notice and respond; that delay is the distributed lag part of the model's name.

    Y, P and O, the ordinary economy

    Three of the project's own series: Y is income per person, P is consumer prices, O is trade openness. They absorb ordinary economic change, so the aid terms are not given credit for inflation or a good growth year.

    s · St, this island's disaster years

    S comes from this ledger: 1 in the coloured years of the grid above, 0 otherwise. Disasters pull money in from families abroad and from donors at once, so S lets the model account for those years honestly instead of mistaking a disaster response for the ordinary relationship between the flows. This is the variation the model keeps.

    g · Gt, the world's crisis years

    G covers what the whole world felt at once. The model is estimated twice, once without G and once with it, under a rule fixed in advance: if the aid terms only matter before G is included, what looked like a relationship was the world, and the project says so. This is the variation the model removes.

    et, what is left over

    Everything the equation cannot explain in year t. The model's checks test that this remainder behaves like noise; if it still contains a pattern, the model is missing something and is not reported as it stands.

    How the estimation runs, in the order the project fixed

    0
    The common shock pre-test. Before anything else, the aid and remittance series are conditioned on G, and a decision rule fixed in advance says what each outcome means. If the aid terms survive conditioning, the programme estimates the relationship as designed. If they collapse, what the literature calls substitution was shock covariance, and the strand reports that instead. This step decides what every later step is allowed to claim.
    1
    Plot everything before testing anything. Every series is drawn, and the S years from this ledger are marked on the plots, so that when a later test reports something strange, the eye can check whether a real event sits underneath it.
    2
    Ask whether each series wanders or returns. Some series come back to a level after a shock and some wander, making every shock permanent. The tests are run three ways and reported in full, because the method in step 6 is chosen precisely for coping with a mixture of the two.
    3
    Test again allowing for a break, because a series that looks like it wanders may instead have snapped once, in a cyclone year or a policy year. The break test hands back a year, and that year is read against this ledger to see whether something real happened then.
    4
    Check nothing wanders twice over, which would make the method invalid. Taking logarithms usually settles it, and the result is written down either way.
    5
    Choose how many lags, which for samples of 44, 31 and 23 years means at most two. A small sample supports a small model, and every reported number travels with the sample size that produced it.
    6
    The bounds test: does a long-run relationship between the flows exist at all? It is the one question everything else waits on, and it is run with critical values computed for samples this small (Narayan 2005) rather than the textbook ones, because the textbook values find relationships that are not there at 44, 31 and 23 observations.
    7
    Estimate the error correction model. One equation produces both answers. The coefficients on this year's changes are the short run, read directly. The long run is computed from the coefficients on last year's levels: if last year's remittances carry 0.04 and last year's outcome carries −0.35, the long-run coefficient is 0.04 divided by 0.35, which is 0.114. The −0.35 is itself a finding twice over. It must be negative and statistically significant, or there is no stable long-run relationship whatever the bounds test said, and its size is the speed at which the system returns toward its long-run path each year, so it says how many years a shock takes to work through.
    8
    Run every diagnostic, every time, including the stability check across the whole sample (CUSUM), because a 45-year window spanning aid regimes, mobile money and COVID has no business being assumed stable. A coefficient without its tests is a number, not a result, and a model that fails them is fixed or reported as failed, never quietly kept.
    9
    Report the sample and what it costs. Then the asymmetric version splits aid rises from aid falls and asks whether a fall hurts more than an equal rise helps, where the sample can carry it.

    Three things to hold on to when reading any result

    First, moving together is not proof that one flow causes the other, and the project's write-ups use the words "moves with", never "causes". Second, the three countries are estimated separately and reported side by side, because averaging Jamaica, Tonga and Comoros would produce a number that describes none of them. Third, every threshold, rule and decision on this page was fixed before any estimate was seen, which is what stops the results being chosen after the fact.
